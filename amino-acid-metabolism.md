### Amino acid overview

```yaml
title: Amino acid overview
category: amino acid metabolism
system: metabolism
high_yield: true
tags: [essential-amino-acids, glucogenic, ketogenic, transamination, ammonia, glucose-alanine-cycle]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Which two amino acids are purely ketogenic?"
    a: "Leucine and lysine."
  - q: "What cofactor do transaminases (ALT/AST) require?"
    a: "Vitamin B6 (pyridoxal phosphate, PLP)."
  - q: "What enzyme releases free ammonia from glutamate?"
    a: "Glutamate dehydrogenase."
  - q: "How is ammonia carried to the liver for disposal?"
    a: "As glutamine (from peripheral tissues) and alanine (from muscle, via the glucose-alanine cycle)."
  - q: "How are the essential amino acids remembered?"
    a: "PVT TIM HaLL (Phe, Val, Thr, Trp, Ile, Met, His, Leu, Lys)."
  - q: "Trap: which cycle carries muscle nitrogen to the liver?"
    a: "The glucose-alanine cycle."
references:
  - "LIR §Amino acid metabolism"   # primary (biochemistry)
```

**Concept.** Amino acids are building blocks for protein and sources of carbon and nitrogen.
**Essential** amino acids can't be synthesized and must come from the diet — remembered as
"PVT TIM HaLL" (Phenylalanine, Valine, Threonine, Tryptophan, Isoleucine, Methionine, Histidine,
Leucine, Lysine). By metabolic fate they are glucogenic (→ gluconeogenic intermediates),
ketogenic (→ acetyl-CoA/acetoacetate), or both; the **only purely ketogenic** ones are **leucine
and lysine**. Nitrogen handling: transaminases (aminotransferases such as ALT and AST, which
require **vitamin B6 as pyridoxal phosphate, PLP**) transfer amino groups to α-ketoglutarate to
form glutamate, and **glutamate dehydrogenase** then releases free ammonia. Ammonia is toxic
(especially to the CNS), so it's carried to the liver as **glutamine** (from peripheral tissues)
and **alanine** (from muscle, via the glucose-alanine cycle) for disposal in the urea cycle.

**Key facts.**
- Essential amino acids (diet): PVT TIM HaLL.
- Glucogenic vs ketogenic; only purely ketogenic = leucine and lysine.
- Transaminases (ALT/AST) need vitamin B6 (PLP); transfer amino groups to α-ketoglutarate → glutamate.
- Glutamate dehydrogenase releases free ammonia.
- Ammonia carried as glutamine (tissues) and alanine (muscle, glucose-alanine cycle) to the liver.

**Clinical correlation.** Ammonia is neurotoxic; defects in nitrogen disposal (urea cycle) cause
hyperammonemia. AST/ALT are clinical markers of hepatocyte injury.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Leucine and lysine are the purely ketogenic amino acids.
- Transamination requires vitamin B6 (PLP).
- The glucose-alanine cycle carries muscle nitrogen to the liver.

**Vignette signature.** "The two purely ketogenic amino acids" → leucine and lysine. "Cofactor for
transaminases" → vitamin B6 (PLP).

**Mnemonic.** Essential = "PVT TIM HaLL"; purely ketogenic = "**L**eucine and **L**ysine (the only
pure L's)."

### Urea cycle & OTC deficiency

```yaml
title: Urea cycle & OTC deficiency
category: nitrogen metabolism
system: metabolism
high_yield: true
tags: [urea-cycle, CPS-I, ornithine-transcarbamylase, hyperammonemia, orotic-acid, x-linked]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Rate-limiting enzyme of the urea cycle, and its activator?"
    a: "Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I (CPS-I), mitochondrial; activated by N-acetylglutamate."
  - q: "Most common urea cycle disorder, and its inheritance?"
    a: "Ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) deficiency; X-linked recessive (the others are autosomal recessive)."
  - q: "Classic lab findings in OTC deficiency?"
    a: "Elevated orotic acid, hyperammonemia, low BUN, and NO megaloblastic anemia."
  - q: "How does OTC deficiency differ from hereditary orotic aciduria?"
    a: "OTC = ↑orotic acid + hyperammonemia (no anemia); orotic aciduria = ↑orotic acid + megaloblastic anemia, no hyperammonemia."
  - q: "Trap: CPS-I vs CPS-II?"
    a: "CPS-I = urea cycle (mitochondrial, uses ammonia); CPS-II = pyrimidine synthesis (cytosolic, uses glutamine)."
  - q: "Vignette: neonate with hyperammonemia, elevated orotic acid, low BUN, no anemia — diagnosis?"
    a: "OTC deficiency."
references:
  - "LIR §Urea cycle"   # primary (biochemistry)
```

**Concept.** The urea cycle, in the liver (partly mitochondrial, partly cytosolic), converts toxic
ammonia into water-soluble urea for excretion. The committed, rate-limiting step is **carbamoyl
phosphate synthetase I (CPS-I)** in the mitochondrion, which combines ammonia and CO₂ (activated by
N-acetylglutamate). The most common urea cycle disorder is **ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC)
deficiency**, which is **X-linked recessive** (the other urea cycle defects are autosomal
recessive). In OTC deficiency, carbamoyl phosphate accumulates and spills into pyrimidine
synthesis, so the classic finding is **elevated orotic acid with hyperammonemia and low BUN, but
no megaloblastic anemia** (which distinguishes it from hereditary orotic aciduria). Hyperammonemia
presents with vomiting, lethargy, and cerebral edema; treatment lowers ammonia (protein
restriction, lactulose, and ammonia scavengers such as benzoate/phenylbutyrate).

**Key facts.**
- Urea cycle (liver) detoxifies ammonia → urea; rate-limiting CPS-I (mitochondrial; needs N-acetylglutamate).
- OTC deficiency = most common urea cycle disorder; X-linked recessive (others AR).
- OTC deficiency: ↑ orotic acid, ↑ ammonia, ↓ BUN; NO megaloblastic anemia.
- Hyperammonemia → vomiting, lethargy, cerebral edema; treat with low protein, lactulose,
  benzoate/phenylbutyrate.

**Clinical correlation.** A neonate/infant with hyperammonemia and elevated orotic acid suggests
OTC deficiency; contrast hereditary orotic aciduria (megaloblastic anemia, *no* hyperammonemia).

**Differentiating traps.**
- OTC deficiency is the only X-linked urea cycle disorder and the most common.
- ↑orotic acid + hyperammonemia (OTC) vs ↑orotic acid + megaloblastic anemia + no hyperammonemia
  (orotic aciduria).
- CPS-I (urea cycle, mitochondrial, uses ammonia) vs CPS-II (pyrimidines, cytosolic, uses glutamine).

**Vignette signature.** "Neonate with hyperammonemia, elevated orotic acid, low BUN, and no anemia"
→ OTC deficiency.

**Mnemonic.** "OTC = X-linked; **o**rotic acid up, ammonia up, BUN down."

### Phenylketonuria (PKU)

```yaml

cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Which enzyme is deficient in classic PKU (and the alternative cause)?"
    a: "Phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH); less commonly its cofactor tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4)."
  - q: "What accumulates and what odor results in PKU?"
    a: "Phenylalanine accumulates and is diverted to phenylketones → a musty/mousy odor."
  - q: "Untreated PKU features?"
    a: "Severe intellectual disability, seizures, hypopigmentation (less melanin), and eczema."
  - q: "PKU treatment, and which amino acid becomes essential?"
    a: "Low-phenylalanine diet (avoid aspartame); tyrosine becomes essential."
  - q: "Why is maternal PKU dangerous?"
    a: "It's teratogenic — microcephaly, congenital heart disease, and intellectual disability in the infant."
  - q: "Trap: classic vs malignant PKU?"
    a: "Classic = PAH defect; malignant = BH4 cofactor defect (also impairs neurotransmitter synthesis)."
references:
  - "LIR §Phenylketonuria"   # primary (biochemistry)
```

**Concept.** Phenylketonuria is autosomal recessive deficiency of **phenylalanine hydroxylase
(PAH)**, which converts phenylalanine to tyrosine — or, less commonly, deficiency of its cofactor
**tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4)**. Phenylalanine accumulates and is diverted to phenylketones,
producing a musty/mousy body odor. Untreated: severe intellectual disability, seizures, and
hypopigmentation (less tyrosine → less melanin) with fair skin/hair and eczema. It's detected on
newborn screening. Treatment is a low-phenylalanine diet (and avoiding **aspartame**, which
contains phenylalanine); tyrosine becomes an essential amino acid. BH4-deficient ("malignant") PKU
also impairs neurotransmitter synthesis and needs extra therapy. Uncontrolled **maternal PKU** is
teratogenic (microcephaly, congenital heart disease, intellectual disability in the infant).

**Key facts.**
- PAH deficiency (or BH4 cofactor deficiency); AR.
- ↑phenylalanine; musty/mousy odor; intellectual disability, seizures, hypopigmentation, eczema.
- Newborn screen; treat with low-phenylalanine diet, avoid aspartame; tyrosine becomes essential.
- Maternal PKU is teratogenic (microcephaly, congenital heart disease).

**Clinical correlation.** A musty-smelling, fair-skinned infant with developmental delay if
untreated; counsel on aspartame avoidance.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Classic PKU = PAH; "malignant" PKU = BH4 cofactor (also impairs neurotransmitters).
- Tyrosine is essential in PKU.
- Maternal PKU harms the fetus.

**Vignette signature.** "Infant with a musty odor, fair skin, and developmental delay, treated with
a low-phenylalanine diet" → PKU.

**Mnemonic.** "PKU: **P**henylalanine can't become tyrosine → musty odor; avoid aspartame."

**Check.**
1. Which enzyme is deficient in classic phenylketonuria? *(→ key)*
2. Which amino acid becomes essential in PKU? *(→ key)*


**Concept.** Alkaptonuria is autosomal recessive deficiency of **homogentisate oxidase** in the
tyrosine degradation pathway, causing homogentisic acid to accumulate. The urine turns **black on
standing** (oxidized homogentisic acid), and over years the pigment deposits in connective tissue
(**ochronosis**), causing bluish-black discoloration of cartilage (ears, sclera) and a debilitating
arthritis of the spine and large joints. Aside from the arthritis, it is relatively benign.

**Key facts.**
- Homogentisate oxidase deficiency; AR; tyrosine degradation pathway.
- Homogentisic acid accumulates → black urine on standing.
- Ochronosis (dark connective tissue/cartilage), arthritis of spine and large joints.
- Otherwise relatively benign.

**Clinical correlation.** Dark urine, dark ear/scleral cartilage, and arthritis in adulthood.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Homogentisate oxidase deficiency; urine darkens on standing or with alkali.
- Ochronosis and arthritis are the main morbidity.

**Vignette signature.** "Urine turns black on standing, with dark ear cartilage/sclera and
arthritis" → alkaptonuria.

**Mnemonic.** "Alka**P**tonuria → blac**K** urine and ochronosis."

### Albinism

```yaml
title: Albinism
category: amino acid metabolism
system: metabolism
enzyme: tyrosinase (most common)
high_yield: true
tags: [albinism, tyrosinase, melanin, hypopigmentation, skin-cancer, nystagmus]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Which enzyme deficiency most commonly causes oculocutaneous albinism?"
    a: "Tyrosinase (converts tyrosine via DOPA to melanin); autosomal recessive."
  - q: "Clinical features of albinism?"
    a: "Hypopigmented skin/hair/eyes, increased skin cancer risk, nystagmus, photophobia, decreased acuity."
  - q: "Trap: albinism vs PKU hypopigmentation mechanism?"
    a: "Albinism = a melanin-synthesis enzyme (tyrosinase) defect; PKU = low tyrosine availability."
  - q: "Vignette: generalized hypopigmentation with nystagmus and photophobia — diagnosis?"
    a: "Albinism (tyrosinase deficiency)."
references:
  - "LIR §Tyrosine metabolism"   # primary (biochemistry)
```

**Concept.** Oculocutaneous albinism is most commonly autosomal recessive deficiency of
**tyrosinase**, the enzyme that converts tyrosine (via DOPA) into melanin. Without melanin there's
hypopigmentation of skin, hair, and eyes, with increased UV-related skin cancer risk and ocular
problems (nystagmus, photophobia, decreased acuity). Tyrosinase-positive forms and defects of
tyrosine transport also occur, and ocular albinism can be X-linked. Distinguish it from PKU's
hypopigmentation, which reflects low tyrosine availability rather than a melanin-synthesis enzyme
defect.

**Key facts.**
- Most common: tyrosinase deficiency; AR (oculocutaneous).
- ↓melanin → hypopigmented skin/hair/eyes; increased skin cancer risk; nystagmus, photophobia.
- A defect in melanin synthesis (tyrosine → melanin), not in tyrosine availability.

**Clinical correlation.** Hypopigmentation with photophobia, sun sensitivity, and elevated skin
cancer risk.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Albinism = tyrosinase (a melanin-synthesis defect); PKU hypopigmentation = low tyrosine.
- It can also result from defective tyrosine transport.

**Vignette signature.** "Generalized hypopigmentation of skin/hair/eyes with nystagmus and
photophobia" → albinism (tyrosinase).

**Mnemonic.** "Albinism: no tyrosinase → no melanin."

```yaml
    a: "Isoleucine, leucine, and valine (the branched-chain amino acids)."
  - q: "What is the characteristic finding in MSUD?"

  - q: "Which vitamin does the MSUD enzyme require?"

```

**Concept.** Maple syrup urine disease is autosomal recessive deficiency of **branched-chain
α-ketoacid dehydrogenase**, which degrades the branched-chain amino acids **isoleucine, leucine,
and valine**. These amino acids and their α-ketoacids accumulate, causing severe CNS toxicity. A
newborn presents with poor feeding, vomiting, and lethargy progressing to neurologic
deterioration, with urine that smells of **maple syrup / burnt sugar**. The enzyme requires
**thiamine (vitamin B1)**. Treatment restricts branched-chain amino acids (with thiamine in some
forms).

**Key facts.**
- Branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase deficiency; AR.
- Can't degrade branched-chain amino acids: isoleucine, leucine, valine.
- Accumulated amino/keto acids → CNS toxicity; sweet, maple-syrup–smelling urine.
- Enzyme needs thiamine (B1); treat by restricting branched-chain amino acids.

**Clinical correlation.** A neonate with sweet-smelling urine, feeding difficulty, and neurologic
signs.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Branched-chain amino acids = isoleucine, leucine, valine (leucine is purely ketogenic).
- A thiamine-dependent enzyme.
- "Maple syrup" odor (vs the musty odor of PKU).

**Vignette signature.** "Newborn with maple-syrup/sweet-smelling urine, vomiting, and neurologic
decline" → MSUD.

**Mnemonic.** "MSUD: can't break the branched — **I L**ove **V**ermont (Isoleucine, Leucine,
Valine); maple syrup urine."

```yaml
    a: "Downward and inward (vs upward and outward in Marfan)."
  - q: "What dangerous feature distinguishes homocystinuria from Marfan?"

  - q: "Other features of homocystinuria?"
    a: "Marfanoid habitus (tall, long limbs) and intellectual disability."
  - q: "Treatment of homocystinuria?"

    a: "Homocystinuria."
references:
  - "LIR §Homocysteine and methionine metabolism"   # primary (biochemistry)
```

**Concept.** Homocystinuria is most commonly autosomal recessive deficiency of **cystathionine
β-synthase (CBS)**, which converts homocysteine to cystathionine (using vitamin B6), so
homocysteine accumulates. The picture resembles Marfan but with key differences: a marfanoid
habitus (tall, long limbs), **lens dislocation that is downward and inward** (vs upward and outward
in Marfan), intellectual disability, and — importantly — **premature atherosclerosis and
thrombosis** (stroke, MI) from homocysteine-mediated vascular damage. Treatment depends on type:
many cases respond to high-dose **vitamin B6 (pyridoxine)**; others need methionine restriction
plus cysteine/betaine. (Homocysteine also rises with B12 or folate deficiency.)

**Key facts.**
- Cystathionine β-synthase (CBS) deficiency; AR; ↑homocysteine.
- Marfanoid habitus, lens dislocation (down/in), intellectual disability.
- Premature atherosclerosis and thrombosis (stroke/MI) — the dangerous feature.
- Treat: high-dose vitamin B6 (some), methionine restriction, cysteine/betaine.

**Clinical correlation.** A young patient with marfanoid features, downward lens dislocation, and
thrombotic events.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Lens dislocation is **downward** (homocystinuria) vs **upward** (Marfan).
- Thrombosis/atherosclerosis distinguishes it from Marfan (aortic dissection from fibrillin).
- CBS uses vitamin B6; many cases are B6-responsive.

**Vignette signature.** "Marfanoid young patient with downward lens dislocation and a thrombotic
stroke" → homocystinuria.

**Mnemonic.** "Homocystinuria: lens goes **DOWN**, and clots take you down; often B6-responsive."

**Check.**
1. Which enzyme is most commonly deficient in homocystinuria? *(→ key)*

**Concept.** Cystinuria is an autosomal recessive defect in the renal (and intestinal) transporter
for the dibasic amino acids — **cystine, ornithine, lysine, and arginine (COLA)** — impairing their
reabsorption. The clinically important consequence is poorly soluble **cystine** in the urine,
which precipitates into recurrent **cystine kidney stones** (often staghorn), classically
beginning in childhood or young adulthood. The crystals are **hexagonal**, and the
cyanide-nitroprusside test is positive. Treatment: hydration, urinary alkalinization, and chelating
agents (penicillamine) to increase cystine solubility.

**Key facts.**
- AR defect in the dibasic amino acid transporter (COLA: Cystine, Ornithine, Lysine, Arginine).
- Cystine is poorly soluble → recurrent cystine kidney stones (hexagonal crystals).
- Positive cyanide-nitroprusside test.
- Treat: hydration, urinary alkalinization, penicillamine.

**Clinical correlation.** Recurrent kidney stones from a young age, with hexagonal crystals on
urinalysis.

**Differentiating traps.**
- A transport defect, not an enzyme deficiency.
- COLA amino acids; the clinically significant stone is cystine (hexagonal).
- Cyanide-nitroprusside positive.

**Vignette signature.** "Recurrent kidney stones since childhood with hexagonal urinary crystals" →
cystinuria.

**Mnemonic.** "Cystinuria: can't reabsorb **COLA**; hexagonal cystine stones."

### Hartnup disease

```yaml
title: Hartnup disease
category: amino acid metabolism
system: metabolism

flashcards:
  - q: "What is the defect in Hartnup disease?"
    a: "An AR defect in the neutral amino acid transporter (intestine/kidney), losing tryptophan."
  - q: "Why does Hartnup disease cause a pellagra-like picture?"
    a: "Tryptophan is a precursor for niacin (B3); its loss → niacin deficiency → photosensitive dermatitis and ataxia."
  - q: "Treatment of Hartnup disease?"
    a: "A high-protein diet plus niacin (nicotinic acid)."
  - q: "Vignette: pellagra-like dermatitis and ataxia with neutral aminoaciduria — diagnosis?"
    a: "Hartnup disease."
references:
  - "LIR §Amino acid transport disorders"   # primary (biochemistry)
```

**Concept.** Hartnup disease is an autosomal recessive defect in the **neutral amino acid
transporter** in the intestine and kidney, impairing absorption/reabsorption of neutral amino acids
— most importantly **tryptophan**. Because tryptophan is a precursor for **niacin (vitamin B3, via
NAD)**, the deficiency produces a **pellagra-like** picture: photosensitive dermatitis, ataxia and
other neurologic symptoms, and neutral aminoaciduria. It is often mild or asymptomatic; treatment
is a high-protein diet plus nicotinic acid (niacin) supplementation.

**Key facts.**
- AR defect in the neutral amino acid transporter (intestine/kidney); loses tryptophan.
- ↓tryptophan → ↓niacin → pellagra-like (dermatitis, ataxia/neuro signs), neutral aminoaciduria.
- Treat with a high-protein diet and niacin (nicotinic acid).

**Clinical correlation.** A pellagra-like rash and ataxia in a child with neutral aminoaciduria.

**Differentiating traps.**
- A transport defect (tryptophan) producing *secondary* niacin deficiency (links to Pellagra/B3).
- Distinguish from dietary niacin deficiency and carcinoid syndrome (which also consumes tryptophan).

**Vignette signature.** "Pellagra-like dermatitis and ataxia with neutral aminoaciduria" → Hartnup
disease.

**Mnemonic.** "Hartnup: lose tryptophan → low niacin → pellagra-like."

|---|---|---|

## Section answer key

**Amino acid overview**
1. Leucine and lysine.
2. Vitamin B6 (pyridoxal phosphate).

**Urea cycle & OTC deficiency**
1. Ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) deficiency.
2. OTC deficiency causes hyperammonemia with elevated orotic acid; hereditary orotic aciduria has
   elevated orotic acid with megaloblastic anemia but *no* hyperammonemia.

**Phenylketonuria (PKU)**
1. Phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH).
2. Tyrosine.

**Alkaptonuria**
1. Homogentisate oxidase.
2. It turns black.

**Albinism**
1. Tyrosinase.
2. Albinism is a melanin-synthesis enzyme (tyrosinase) defect; PKU hypopigmentation results from
   low tyrosine availability.

**Maple syrup urine disease (MSUD)**
1. Branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase.
2. Isoleucine, leucine, and valine.

**Homocystinuria**
1. Cystathionine β-synthase (CBS).
2. Downward (and inward) — versus upward/outward in Marfan.

**Cystinuria**
1. The dibasic amino acids — cystine, ornithine, lysine, arginine (COLA).
2. Hexagonal.

**Hartnup disease**
1. Tryptophan.
2. Niacin (vitamin B3).

**Amino acid disorder summary**
1. Cystinuria.
2. Maple syrup urine disease (MSUD).
