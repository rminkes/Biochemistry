### Vitamin B1 (thiamine)

```yaml
title: Vitamin B1 (thiamine)
category: water-soluble vitamin
system: nutrition
high_yield: true
tags: [thiamine, TPP, wernicke, korsakoff, beriberi, alcoholism]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Active form of thiamine, and which four enzymes use it?"
    a: "Thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP); pyruvate dehydrogenase, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase, and transketolase."
  - q: "Classic triad of Wernicke encephalopathy?"
    a: "Confusion, ophthalmoplegia, and ataxia."
  - q: "Wernicke vs Korsakoff?"
    a: "Wernicke (confusion, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia) progresses untreated to Korsakoff (memory loss, confabulation, often irreversible)."
  - q: "Dry vs wet beriberi?"
    a: "Dry = peripheral neuropathy; wet = high-output cardiac failure/cardiomyopathy with edema."
  - q: "Most common cause of thiamine deficiency in developed countries?"
    a: "Alcoholism."
  - q: "Why give thiamine before glucose?"
    a: "A glucose load can precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy."
references:
  - "LIR §Thiamine"   # primary (biochemistry)
```

**Concept.** Thiamine is converted to **thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP)**, a cofactor for key
dehydrogenase and transketolase reactions: **pyruvate dehydrogenase**, **α-ketoglutarate
dehydrogenase** (TCA cycle), **branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase**, and **transketolase**
(pentose phosphate pathway). Deficiency impairs glucose-derived ATP, hitting highly aerobic tissues
(brain, heart). Causes: alcoholism (the most common cause in developed countries) and malnutrition.
Syndromes: **Wernicke encephalopathy** (triad of confusion, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia), which —
untreated — progresses to **Korsakoff syndrome** (memory loss, confabulation, often irreversible);
**dry beriberi** (peripheral neuropathy); and **wet beriberi** (high-output cardiac
failure/cardiomyopathy with edema). Give **thiamine before glucose** in suspected deficiency (a
glucose load can precipitate Wernicke).

**Key facts.**
- Active form: thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP); cofactor for pyruvate dehydrogenase, α-ketoglutarate
  dehydrogenase, branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase, transketolase.
- Deficiency: alcoholism, malnutrition.
- Wernicke (confusion, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia) → Korsakoff (memory loss, confabulation).
- Dry beriberi (neuropathy); wet beriberi (high-output cardiac failure).
- Give thiamine before glucose.

**Clinical correlation.** An alcoholic patient with confusion, ataxia, and ophthalmoplegia — treat
with thiamine first.

**Differentiating traps.**
- TPP serves four enzymes (pyruvate dehydrogenase, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, branched-chain
  α-ketoacid dehydrogenase, transketolase).
- Give thiamine *before* glucose (glucose can precipitate Wernicke).
- Wet (cardiac) vs dry (neuropathy) beriberi.

**Vignette signature.** "Alcoholic with confusion, ophthalmoplegia, and ataxia" → Wernicke
(thiamine deficiency).

**Mnemonic.** "Thiamine enzymes — think **ATP**: **α**-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase,
**T**ransketolase, **P**yruvate dehydrogenase (plus branched-chain)."

```yaml
  - q: "Toxicity of pharmacologic niacin?"

references:
  - "LIR §Niacin"          # primary (biochemistry)
  - "ROBBINS §Pellagra"     # cross-reference (pathology)
```

**Concept.** Niacin is the precursor of **NAD⁺ and NADP⁺**, the central redox carriers, and can be
synthesized from **tryptophan** (which requires vitamin B6). Deficiency causes **pellagra**: the
"3 D's" — **D**ermatitis (photosensitive, e.g., the Casal necklace), **D**iarrhea, and **D**ementia
(with death if untreated). Causes include dietary deficiency, **Hartnup disease** (tryptophan loss),
**carcinoid syndrome** (tryptophan diverted to serotonin), and **isoniazid** (B6 depletion).
Pharmacologic excess (niacin is used for dyslipidemia) causes facial flushing (prostaglandin-
mediated, blunted by aspirin), hyperglycemia, and hyperuricemia.

**Key facts.**
- Precursor of NAD⁺/NADP⁺; can be made from tryptophan (needs B6).
- Deficiency = pellagra: 3 D's — Dermatitis, Diarrhea, Dementia.
- Secondary causes: diet, Hartnup, carcinoid, isoniazid.
- Toxicity: flushing (aspirin helps), hyperglycemia, hyperuricemia.

**Clinical correlation.** Pellagra (3 D's); also seen in carcinoid and Hartnup. Therapeutic niacin
causes flushing.

**Differentiating traps.**
- B3 → NAD/NADP; pellagra's 3 D's.
- Secondary deficiency: Hartnup, carcinoid, isoniazid.
- Toxicity: flushing and hyperuricemia.

**Vignette signature.** "Photosensitive dermatitis, diarrhea, and dementia" → pellagra (niacin
deficiency).

**Mnemonic.** "Pellagra = 3 **D**'s: **D**ermatitis, **D**iarrhea, **D**ementia."

**Concept.** Pyridoxine is converted to **pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)**, a cofactor for transamination

anemia.

**Differentiating traps.**
- PLP for transamination (links to amino acid metabolism) and heme synthesis (ALA synthase).
- Isoniazid → B6 deficiency.
- Deficiency seizures from low GABA.

**Vignette signature.** "Patient on isoniazid develops peripheral neuropathy or seizures" → B6
deficiency.

**Mnemonic.** "B6 (PLP): transamination, neurotransmitters, heme; isoniazid steals B6."

### Vitamin B9 (folate)

```yaml
title: Vitamin B9 (folate)

  - q: "Deficiency picture of folate?"
    a: "Megaloblastic (macrocytic) anemia with hypersegmented neutrophils, but NO neurologic symptoms."
  - q: "Lab pattern in folate deficiency?"
    a: "Elevated homocysteine with normal methylmalonic acid."
  - q: "Why does folate deficiency develop within months?"
    a: "Body stores are small."
  - q: "Causes of folate deficiency?"
    a: "Poor diet, alcoholism, pregnancy, and drugs (methotrexate, trimethoprim, phenytoin)."
  - q: "What does periconceptional folate prevent?"
    a: "Neural tube defects."
references:
  - "LIR §Folic acid"                 # primary (biochemistry)
  - "ROBBINS §Megaloblastic anemia"    # cross-reference (pathology)
```

**Concept.** Folate is converted to **tetrahydrofolate (THF)**, a carrier of one-carbon units for
synthesis of nucleotides (purines and thymidylate) — essential for DNA synthesis and rapidly
dividing cells. It comes from leafy greens, and body stores are small, so deficiency develops within
months. Deficiency causes **megaloblastic (macrocytic) anemia** with hypersegmented neutrophils,
but **without neurologic symptoms** — the key contrast with B12. Causes: poor diet, alcoholism,
pregnancy (increased demand), and drugs (methotrexate, trimethoprim, phenytoin). Maternal folate
deficiency causes neural tube defects, so it is supplemented periconceptionally. Labs: elevated
**homocysteine** with **normal methylmalonic acid** (in B12 deficiency, both rise).

**Key facts.**
- Active form: tetrahydrofolate (THF); one-carbon carrier for purine/thymidylate synthesis.
- Small stores (deficiency within months); leafy greens.
- Deficiency: megaloblastic anemia, hypersegmented neutrophils, *no* neurologic symptoms.
- Causes: diet, alcohol, pregnancy, methotrexate/trimethoprim/phenytoin.
- ↑homocysteine, normal methylmalonic acid; prevents neural tube defects.

**Clinical correlation.** Megaloblastic anemia without neurologic signs; periconceptional folate
prevents neural tube defects.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Folate deficiency: megaloblastic anemia, *no* neurologic symptoms (vs B12, which has them).
- ↑homocysteine, *normal* methylmalonic acid (B12: both elevated).
- Small stores → rapid depletion.

**Vignette signature.** "Megaloblastic anemia with hypersegmented neutrophils, no neurologic
deficits, elevated homocysteine, normal methylmalonic acid" → folate deficiency.

**Mnemonic.** "Folate: **F**ast depletion, **F**etal neural tube; megaloblastic anemia, no neuro."

### Vitamin B12 (cobalamin)

```yaml
title: Vitamin B12 (cobalamin)
category: water-soluble vitamin
system: nutrition
high_yield: true
tags: [cobalamin, methionine-synthase, methylmalonyl-CoA-mutase, intrinsic-factor, subacute-combined-degeneration, pernicious-anemia, methylmalonic-acid]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Which two enzymes require vitamin B12 as a cofactor?"
    a: "Methionine synthase (homocysteine → methionine) and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase."
  - q: "What is needed for B12 absorption, and where does it occur?"
    a: "Intrinsic factor (from gastric parietal cells); absorbed in the terminal ileum."
  - q: "Deficiency picture of B12?"
    a: "Megaloblastic anemia WITH neurologic symptoms — subacute combined degeneration."
  - q: "Lab discriminator of B12 from folate deficiency?"
    a: "Methylmalonic acid is elevated in B12 deficiency (both raise homocysteine)."
  - q: "Causes of B12 deficiency?"
    a: "Pernicious anemia, terminal ileal disease, Diphyllobothrium latum, vegan diet, pancreatic insufficiency."
  - q: "What does giving folate alone do in a B12-deficient patient?"
    a: "It corrects the anemia but not the neurologic damage (and may worsen it)."
references:
  - "LIR §Cobalamin"                  # primary (biochemistry)
  - "ROBBINS §Pernicious anemia"       # cross-reference (pathology)
```

**Concept.** Cobalamin is a cofactor for two reactions: **methionine synthase** (homocysteine →
methionine, regenerating THF from methyl-THF — the "methyl trap") and **methylmalonyl-CoA mutase**
(methylmalonyl-CoA → succinyl-CoA). Absorption requires **intrinsic factor** (from gastric parietal
cells) and occurs in the **terminal ileum**; large hepatic stores mean deficiency takes years.
Deficiency causes **megaloblastic anemia with neurologic symptoms** — **subacute combined
degeneration** of the dorsal columns and lateral corticospinal/spinocerebellar tracts
(paresthesias, ataxia, loss of proprioception/vibration), from impaired myelin (methylmalonic acid
accumulation). Causes: **pernicious anemia** (autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor), terminal ileal
disease (Crohn's, resection), *Diphyllobothrium latum*, vegan diet, and pancreatic insufficiency.
Labs: **both homocysteine and methylmalonic acid are elevated** (methylmalonic acid is the
discriminator from folate). Giving folate to a B12-deficient patient corrects the anemia but **not
the neurologic damage** (and may worsen it).

**Key facts.**
- Cofactor for methionine synthase (homocysteine → methionine; regenerates THF) and
  methylmalonyl-CoA mutase.
- Absorption: intrinsic factor (gastric parietal cells), terminal ileum; large stores (deficiency
  over years).
- Deficiency: megaloblastic anemia *with* neuro (subacute combined degeneration); ↑homocysteine
  *and* ↑methylmalonic acid.
- Causes: pernicious anemia, ileal disease, *Diphyllobothrium latum*, vegan diet.
- Folate corrects the anemia but not the neuro damage in B12 deficiency.

**Clinical correlation.** Megaloblastic anemia plus neurologic signs (loss of proprioception/
vibration, ataxia); pernicious anemia.

**Differentiating traps.**
- B12 deficiency: megaloblastic anemia *with* neuro (subacute combined degeneration).
- Both homocysteine and methylmalonic acid elevated (methylmalonic acid distinguishes it from folate).
- Folate masks the B12 anemia but not the neurologic disease.

**Vignette signature.** "Megaloblastic anemia *with* neuropathy/ataxia/proprioception loss and
elevated methylmalonic acid" → B12 deficiency.

**Mnemonic.** "B12: **B**ig stores, neuro (methylmalonic acid up); folate alone won't fix the nerves."

- Vitamin C drives collagen *hydroxylation* (distinct from the *cross-linking* step, which uses

### Vitamin A (retinol)

```yaml
title: Vitamin A (retinol)
category: fat-soluble vitamin
system: nutrition
high_yield: true
tags: [vitamin-A, retinol, retinal, rhodopsin, night-blindness, teratogen, isotretinoin, APL]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Roles of vitamin A?"
    a: "Vision (retinal in rhodopsin), epithelial differentiation/integrity, immunity, and growth."
  - q: "Earliest sign of vitamin A deficiency?"
    a: "Night blindness (nyctalopia)."
  - q: "Other deficiency findings of vitamin A?"
    a: "Xerophthalmia (dry eyes), Bitot spots, keratomalacia, and impaired immunity."
  - q: "Toxicity of vitamin A?"
    a: "Acute — ↑ICP/pseudotumor cerebri, nausea; chronic — alopecia, dry skin, hepatotoxicity; and teratogenic."
  - q: "Why avoid vitamin A / isotretinoin in pregnancy?"
    a: "They are teratogenic (cause birth defects)."
  - q: "Therapeutic uses of retinoids?"
    a: "All-trans retinoic acid for acute promyelocytic leukemia; isotretinoin for acne; vitamin A for measles in deficiency."
references:
  - "LIR §Fat-soluble vitamins (A)"   # primary (biochemistry)
```

**Concept.** Vitamin A (retinol) and its derivatives (retinal, retinoic acid) are essential for
vision (**retinal** is part of **rhodopsin** in rod cells), epithelial differentiation and
integrity, immune function, and growth. Sources: liver and leafy/orange vegetables (β-carotene).
Deficiency: **night blindness (nyctalopia — the earliest sign)**, xerophthalmia (dry eyes), Bitot
spots (conjunctival), keratomalacia (corneal degeneration), and impaired immunity with squamous
metaplasia. Toxicity (it's stored, being fat-soluble): acute — nausea, vomiting, and raised
intracranial pressure (ICP) / pseudotumor cerebri; chronic — alopecia, dry skin, hepatotoxicity;
and, critically, **teratogenicity** (isotretinoin and high-dose vitamin A cause birth defects, so
pregnancy must be excluded). Therapeutic uses: acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL — all-trans
retinoic acid), acne (isotretinoin), and measles in deficiency.

**Key facts.**
- Retinol/retinal/retinoic acid: vision (retinal in rhodopsin), epithelial differentiation,
  immunity, growth.
- Deficiency: night blindness (earliest), xerophthalmia, Bitot spots, keratomalacia, immune impairment.
- Toxicity: acute (↑ICP/pseudotumor cerebri, nausea), chronic (alopecia, dry skin, hepatotoxicity);
  teratogenic.
- Therapeutic: acute promyelocytic leukemia (all-trans retinoic acid), acne (isotretinoin), measles.

**Clinical correlation.** Night blindness in deficiency; teratogenicity with isotretinoin (avoid in
pregnancy).

**Differentiating traps.**
- Night blindness is the earliest deficiency sign (retinal/rhodopsin).
- Teratogenic (isotretinoin); pseudotumor cerebri in toxicity.
- All-trans retinoic acid treats acute promyelocytic leukemia.

**Vignette signature.** "Night blindness, dry eyes, Bitot spots" → vitamin A deficiency. "Birth
defects associated with an acne medication" → vitamin A (isotretinoin) toxicity.

**Mnemonic.** "Vitamin **A** = vision (retin**A**l); toxic A = teratogenic and ↑ICP."

### Vitamin D

```yaml
title: Vitamin D
category: fat-soluble vitamin
system: nutrition
high_yield: true
tags: [vitamin-D, calcitriol, 1-alpha-hydroxylase, rickets, osteomalacia, calcium, PTH]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Two-step activation of vitamin D?"
    a: "Liver adds a 25-hydroxyl (25-OH-D, the storage/measured form), then the kidney's 1α-hydroxylase makes 1,25-(OH)₂-D (calcitriol, active)."
  - q: "What is the active form of vitamin D, and what does it do?"
    a: "Calcitriol (1,25-(OH)₂-D); it increases intestinal absorption of calcium and phosphate."
  - q: "Vitamin D deficiency in children vs adults?"
    a: "Rickets (children — bowed legs, rachitic rosary) and osteomalacia (adults — bone pain)."
  - q: "Lab pattern in vitamin D deficiency?"
    a: "Serum calcium low OR normal (PTH compensates), low phosphate, high PTH, high alkaline phosphatase."
  - q: "Why is serum calcium often normal in vitamin D deficiency?"
    a: "A compensatory rise in PTH maintains it."
  - q: "Why does chronic kidney disease cause functional vitamin D deficiency?"
    a: "Failed renal 1α-hydroxylation."
references:
  - "LIR §Fat-soluble vitamins (D)"   # primary (biochemistry)
  - "ROBBINS §Rickets and osteomalacia"   # cross-reference (pathology)
```

**Concept.** Vitamin D regulates calcium and phosphate for bone mineralization. Sources/synthesis:
**D3 (cholecalciferol)** from skin (UV light) and animal sources; **D2 (ergocalciferol)** from
plants/fungi. Activation is two-step: the **liver** adds a 25-hydroxyl (25-OH-D, the storage form
and the value measured to assess status), then the **kidney** (via **1α-hydroxylase**) makes
**1,25-(OH)₂-D (calcitriol, the active form)**. Calcitriol increases intestinal absorption of
calcium and phosphate and supports bone. Deficiency: **rickets** in children (bowed legs,
costochondral "rachitic rosary," delayed fontanelle closure) and **osteomalacia** in adults
(poorly mineralized bone, bone pain). Labs in deficiency: **serum calcium low or normal** (a
compensatory rise in parathyroid hormone (PTH) maintains it), low phosphate, high PTH, and high
alkaline phosphatase. Causes: low sunlight, malabsorption, chronic kidney disease (CKD — failed
1α-hydroxylation), and liver disease. Toxicity: hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria (from excess
supplementation or granulomatous disease producing calcitriol).

**Key facts.**
- Regulates calcium/phosphate for bone; D3 (skin/animal), D2 (plant).
- Activation: liver 25-hydroxylase (storage 25-OH-D) → kidney 1α-hydroxylase (active 1,25-(OH)₂-D,
  calcitriol).
- Calcitriol ↑ intestinal calcium and phosphate absorption.
- Deficiency: rickets (children), osteomalacia (adults); serum calcium low/normal, low phosphate,
  ↑PTH, ↑alkaline phosphatase.
- Toxicity: hypercalcemia/hypercalciuria.

**Clinical correlation.** Rickets/osteomalacia; CKD impairs renal 1α-hydroxylation. Note that
serum calcium is low *or normal* in deficiency because PTH compensates.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Active form = 1,25-(OH)₂-D (calcitriol); the storage/measured form = 25-OH-D.
- Deficiency calcium is low *or normal* (not necessarily low — PTH compensation).
- CKD → failed renal 1α-hydroxylation.

**Vignette signature.** "Child with bowed legs, rachitic rosary, low/normal calcium, low phosphate,
high PTH and alkaline phosphatase" → vitamin D deficiency (rickets).

**Mnemonic.** "**D**: liver makes 25 (storage), kidney makes 1,25 (active); deficiency →
rickets/osteomalacia."

**Concept.** Vitamin E (tocopherol) is a lipid-soluble **antioxidant** that protects cell membranes
— especially red blood cell membranes and neurons — from free-radical oxidative damage. Deficiency:

**Differentiating traps.**
- The vitamin E neuro picture mimics B12 but has *no* megaloblastic anemia and *normal* methylmalonic

- An antioxidant protecting membranes; deficiency → hemolysis.
- Links to abetalipoproteinemia.

**Vignette signature.** "Neuropathy and ataxia with hemolytic anemia in fat malabsorption, with

**Concept.** Vitamin K is required for the **γ-carboxylation** of glutamate residues on clotting
factors **II, VII, IX, X** and proteins **C and S** — a post-translational modification that lets
these factors bind calcium and function. The enzyme **epoxide reductase** regenerates active vitamin
K and is the target of **warfarin**. Sources: green vegetables and synthesis by gut bacteria.
Deficiency causes bleeding (prolonged PT/PTT) — notably **neonatal hemorrhagic disease** (newborns
have sterile guts and low stores, so vitamin K is given at birth) and deficiency with prolonged
antibiotic use or fat malabsorption.

**Key facts.**
- Cofactor for γ-carboxylation of factors II, VII, IX, X and proteins C, S.
- Activated by epoxide reductase (the target of warfarin).
- Sources: green vegetables and gut bacteria.
- Deficiency: bleeding (↑PT/PTT); neonatal hemorrhagic disease (give vitamin K at birth);
  antibiotics/malabsorption.

**Clinical correlation.** Newborn bleeding when the vitamin K injection is missed; the warfarin
mechanism; bleeding with malabsorption or prolonged antibiotics.

**Differentiating traps.**
- γ-carboxylation of factors II, VII, IX, X plus proteins C and S.
- Warfarin inhibits epoxide reductase.
- Neonates require vitamin K (sterile gut, low stores).

**Vignette signature.** "Newborn with bleeding who did not receive a vitamin K injection" → vitamin
K deficiency. "Drug inhibiting vitamin K epoxide reductase" → warfarin.

**Mnemonic.** "Vitamin **K** = **K**oagulation; factors 2, 7, 9, 10 + proteins C, S; warfarin blocks
epoxide reductase."

**Concept.** This module consolidates all vitamins by cofactor/role, deficiency, and toxicity; each
is detailed in its own module above. Two organizing principles: **water-soluble** vitamins
(B-complex, C) generally are not stored, so deficiency develops relatively quickly and toxicity is
rare — the practical exceptions being B12 (large stores) and the few with meaningful toxicity (B6,
B3); **fat-soluble** vitamins (A, D, E, K) are stored, so deficiency tracks with fat malabsorption
and toxicity genuinely matters (especially A and D).

**Key facts.**
- Fat-soluble: A, D, E, K (stored; toxicity matters; malabsorption causes deficiency).
- Water-soluble: B-complex and C (generally not stored; B12 is the large-store exception).
- Megaloblastic anemia: folate (no neuro) vs B12 (neuro + ↑methylmalonic acid).
- Collagen vitamins: C (hydroxylation) and the cofactor copper for lysyl oxidase cross-linking.

**Clinical correlation.** Match cofactor/role and the characteristic deficiency clue to the vitamin;
remember the fat-soluble toxicities (A teratogen/↑ICP; D hypercalcemia).

**Differentiating traps.**
- Fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) are the ones with clinically important toxicity and malabsorption-linked
  deficiency.
- B12 is the water-soluble vitamin with large stores.

**Vignette signature.** Cofactor/deficiency clue → vitamin (see table).

## Section answer key

*(Vitamins — water-soluble and fat-soluble.)*

**Vitamin B1 (thiamine)**
1. Thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP).
2. Confusion, ophthalmoplegia, and ataxia.

**Vitamin B2 (riboflavin)**
1. FAD and FMN.
2. Cheilosis (angular stomatitis), glossitis, and corneal vascularization.

**Vitamin B3 (niacin)**
1. Pellagra — dermatitis, diarrhea, and dementia.
2. Tryptophan.

**Vitamin B5 (pantothenate)**
1. Coenzyme A (CoA).
2. Fatty acid synthase (the acyl carrier protein).

**Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine)**
1. Pyridoxal phosphate (PLP).
2. Isoniazid.

**Vitamin B7 (biotin)**
1. Carboxylases (pyruvate carboxylase, acetyl-CoA carboxylase, propionyl-CoA carboxylase).
2. Excessive raw egg whites (avidin binds biotin).

**Vitamin B9 (folate)**
1. Tetrahydrofolate (THF).
2. Folate deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia *without* neurologic symptoms, whereas B12
   deficiency adds neurologic disease (subacute combined degeneration).

**Vitamin B12 (cobalamin)**
1. Methionine synthase and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase.
2. Methylmalonic acid.

**Vitamin C (ascorbate / scurvy)**
1. Hydroxylation of proline and lysine.
2. Scurvy.

**Vitamin A (retinol)**
1. Night blindness (nyctalopia).
2. It is teratogenic (causes birth defects).

**Vitamin D**
1. 1,25-(OH)₂-D (calcitriol).
2. The kidney.

**Vitamin E (tocopherol)**
1. A lipid-soluble antioxidant protecting cell membranes (RBCs, neurons).
2. Vitamin E deficiency has no megaloblastic anemia and a normal methylmalonic acid, whereas B12
   deficiency has both.

**Vitamin K**
1. Factors II, VII, IX, and X (and proteins C and S).
2. Newborns have sterile guts (no bacterial vitamin K synthesis) and low stores, putting them at
   risk of hemorrhagic disease.
