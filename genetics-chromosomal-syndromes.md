# Genetics — Chromosomal Syndromes

Section D of the biochem coverage checklist: the recognizable clusters produced by whole-
chromosome aneuploidies and microdeletions — the autosomal trisomies, the sex-chromosome
aneuploidies, and the microdeletion syndromes (including the Prader-Willi/Angelman imprinting
pair whose mechanism is covered in Genetics — Principles & Inheritance).

```yaml
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "What is the most common cause of trisomy 21?"
    a: "Maternal meiotic nondisjunction (risk rises with maternal age)."
  - q: "Which translocation form of Down syndrome carries an age-independent recurrence risk?"
    a: "A Robertsonian translocation (often involving chromosome 14)."
  - q: "Characteristic physical features of Down syndrome?"
    a: "Flat facial profile, epicanthal folds, upslanting palpebral fissures, single transverse palmar (simian) crease, Brushfield spots, hypotonia."
  - q: "Most characteristic congenital heart defect in Down syndrome?"
    a: "AV septal (endocardial cushion) defect."
  - q: "GI associations of Down syndrome?"
    a: "Duodenal atresia (double bubble) and Hirschsprung disease."
  - q: "Why early-onset Alzheimer disease in Down syndrome?"
    a: "The APP gene is on chromosome 21 (gene dosage)."
  - q: "Quad-screen pattern in Down syndrome?"
    a: "↓AFP, ↓estriol, ↑hCG, ↑inhibin A."
references:
  - "THOMPSON §Down syndrome"                 # primary (genetics)
  - "ROBBINS §Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome)"      # cross-reference (pathology)
```

**Concept.** Down syndrome is the most common viable autosomal trisomy and the most common
chromosomal cause of intellectual disability. Most cases arise from **maternal meiotic
nondisjunction** (risk rises with maternal age); a minority come from a **Robertsonian
translocation** (often involving chromosome 14, which can be inherited and carries an
age-independent recurrence risk) or mosaicism. Features include intellectual disability, a flat
facial profile with epicanthal folds and upslanting palpebral fissures, a single transverse
palmar (simian) crease, Brushfield spots, and hypotonia. High-yield associations: congenital
heart disease (the AV septal/endocardial cushion defect is most characteristic), duodenal atresia
("double bubble"), Hirschsprung disease, early-onset Alzheimer disease (the APP gene is on
chromosome 21), and increased risk of acute leukemias (ALL and AML). The maternal quad screen
shows ↓AFP, ↓estriol, ↑hCG, and ↑inhibin A.

**Key facts.**
- Trisomy 21; most common viable autosomal trisomy and chromosomal cause of intellectual disability.
- Usually maternal meiotic nondisjunction (↑ maternal age); also Robertsonian translocation, mosaicism.
- Flat facies, epicanthal folds, simian crease, Brushfield spots, hypotonia.
- AV septal defect, duodenal atresia, Hirschsprung, early Alzheimer (APP), ↑leukemia (ALL/AML).
- Quad screen: ↓AFP, ↓estriol, ↑hCG, ↑inhibin A.

**Clinical correlation.** Early-onset Alzheimer disease reflects the APP gene dosage on chromosome
21; leukemia risk is elevated throughout childhood.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Quad-screen pattern (↓AFP, ↓estriol, ↑hCG, ↑inhibin A) distinguishes it from trisomy 18 (all low).
- The Robertsonian translocation form has an age-independent recurrence risk.

**Vignette signature.** "Flat facies, single palmar crease, AV septal defect, duodenal atresia" →
Down syndrome.

**Mnemonic.** "Down quad screen: only hCG and inhibin go **UP**."

**Concept.** Edwards syndrome (trisomy 18) is the second most common autosomal trisomy, and most
affected infants die within the first year. Features include severe intellectual disability,
micrognathia, a prominent occiput, low-set ears, **clenched hands with overlapping fingers**, and
**rocker-bottom feet**, along with congenital heart disease. The quad screen is **uniformly low**
(↓AFP, ↓estriol, ↓hCG, and low or normal inhibin A).

**Key facts.**
- Trisomy 18; severe; death usually within the first year.
- Micrognathia, prominent occiput, low-set ears, clenched fists with overlapping fingers,
  rocker-bottom feet.
- Congenital heart disease.
- Quad screen: ↓AFP, ↓estriol, ↓hCG, ↓/normal inhibin A.

**Clinical correlation.** Prognosis is poor; management is supportive.

**Differentiating traps.**
- 18 → Edwards ("**E**lection age," 18). Clenched fists + rocker-bottom feet.
- Quad screen all-low, versus Down's mixed pattern.

**Vignette signature.** "Clenched hands with overlapping fingers, rocker-bottom feet,
micrognathia" → Edwards (trisomy 18).

**Mnemonic.** "Edwards = 18 = **E**lection age; clenched fists, rocker-bottom feet."

### Turner syndrome (45,X)

```yaml
title: Turner syndrome (45,X)
category: sex-chromosome aneuploidy
system: genetics
high_yield: true
tags: [45-X, monosomy-X, streak-ovaries, primary-amenorrhea, coarctation, horseshoe-kidney]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Karyotype of Turner syndrome and its most common cause?"
    a: "45,X (monosomy X), usually paternal sex-chromosome nondisjunction — notably not linked to advanced maternal age."
  - q: "Turner syndrome is the most common cause of what?"
    a: "Primary amenorrhea."
  - q: "What causes the amenorrhea and infertility in Turner syndrome?"
    a: "Ovarian dysgenesis (streak ovaries)."
  - q: "Characteristic physical features of Turner syndrome?"
    a: "Short stature, webbed neck (from a fetal cystic hygroma), shield chest with widely spaced nipples, low hairline, newborn hand/foot lymphedema."
  - q: "Cardiovascular and renal associations of Turner syndrome?"
    a: "Bicuspid aortic valve and coarctation of the aorta (arm-leg BP differential); horseshoe kidney."
  - q: "Hormone profile and Barr body status in Turner syndrome?"
    a: "Hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (↑FSH/LH, low estrogen); no Barr body (single X)."
  - q: "Vignette: short female with webbed neck, primary amenorrhea, and coarctation (arm-leg BP gap) — diagnosis?"
    a: "Turner syndrome."
references:
  - "THOMPSON §Turner syndrome"               # primary (genetics)
  - "ROBBINS §Turner syndrome"                 # cross-reference (pathology)
```

**Concept.** Turner syndrome is monosomy X (**45,X**), the most common cause of **primary
amenorrhea**, usually from paternal sex-chromosome nondisjunction (notably *not* linked to
advanced maternal age) or mosaicism. Features: short stature, **ovarian dysgenesis ("streak
ovaries")** causing primary amenorrhea and infertility, a webbed neck (from a fetal cystic
hygroma), shield chest with widely spaced nipples, a low hairline, and lymphedema of the hands
and feet in the newborn. High-yield cardiovascular/renal associations: **bicuspid aortic valve**
and **coarctation of the aorta** (look for a blood-pressure differential between arms and legs)
and **horseshoe kidney**. Hormonally there is hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (↑FSH/LH, low
estrogen), and there is **no Barr body** (a single X).

**Key facts.**
- 45,X (monosomy X); most common cause of primary amenorrhea.
- Short stature, streak ovaries, webbed neck, shield chest, newborn lymphedema.
- Bicuspid aortic valve, coarctation of the aorta, horseshoe kidney.
- ↑FSH/LH, ↓estrogen; no Barr body.

**Clinical correlation.** Coarctation → arm-leg BP differential; horseshoe kidney; treated with
growth hormone and estrogen replacement.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (↑FSH/LH) — the ovary is the problem.
- Coarctation/bicuspid aortic valve point to Turner.
- No Barr body (only one X), versus Klinefelter.

**Vignette signature.** "Short female with webbed neck, primary amenorrhea, and coarctation (arm-
leg BP gap)" → Turner.

**Mnemonic.** "Turner: single X — short, streak ovaries, webbed neck, coarctation."

### Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY)

```yaml
title: Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY)
category: sex-chromosome aneuploidy
system: genetics
high_yield: true
tags: [47-XXY, hypogonadism, gynecomastia, small-firm-testes, hypergonadotropic, barr-body]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Karyotype of Klinefelter syndrome and what it causes?"
    a: "47,XXY; the most common cause of male hypogonadism / primary testicular failure."
  - q: "Hormone profile in Klinefelter syndrome?"
    a: "Low testosterone, high FSH/LH (hypergonadotropic), low inhibin B; elevated estrogen-to-androgen ratio."
  - q: "Characteristic features of Klinefelter syndrome?"
    a: "Tall stature with long extremities, gynecomastia, small firm testes, infertility (azoospermia)."
  - q: "Why does gynecomastia occur in Klinefelter syndrome?"
    a: "An elevated estrogen-to-androgen ratio."
  - q: "Barr body in Klinefelter vs Turner?"
    a: "Present in Klinefelter (the extra X is inactivated); absent in Turner."
  - q: "Vignette: tall male with gynecomastia, small firm testes, infertility, and high FSH/LH — diagnosis?"
    a: "Klinefelter syndrome."
references:
  - "THOMPSON §Klinefelter syndrome"   # primary (genetics)
```

**Concept.** Klinefelter syndrome (**47,XXY**) is the most common cause of male hypogonadism and
primary testicular failure. The extra X causes seminiferous tubule dysgenesis and Leydig cell
dysfunction, so **testosterone is low and FSH/LH are high** (hypergonadotropic), with low inhibin
B. Features emerge at or after puberty: **tall stature with long extremities**, **gynecomastia**,
**small firm testes**, and infertility (azoospermia), often with subtle learning/behavioral
issues. The elevated **estrogen-to-androgen ratio** drives the gynecomastia and feminization. A
**Barr body is present** (the inactivated extra X), and breast cancer risk is increased.

**Key facts.**
- 47,XXY; most common cause of male hypogonadism / primary testicular failure.
- Small firm testes, gynecomastia, tall stature with long extremities, infertility.
- ↓testosterone, ↑FSH/LH (hypergonadotropic), ↓inhibin B; ↑estrogen:androgen ratio.
- Barr body present; increased breast cancer risk.

**Clinical correlation.** Presents at puberty with incomplete virilization, gynecomastia, and
infertility; treated with testosterone replacement.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (↑FSH/LH) — testicular failure.
- Barr body present (extra X inactivated), versus Turner (none).
- Gynecomastia reflects the high estrogen:androgen ratio.

**Vignette signature.** "Tall male with gynecomastia, small firm testes, infertility, and high
FSH/LH" → Klinefelter.

**Mnemonic.** "Klinefelter = XX**Y**; the e**X**tra X → gynecomastia, small testes."

**Check.**
1. What is the karyotype of Klinefelter syndrome? *(→ key)*
2. Are gonadotropins (FSH/LH) high or low in Klinefelter syndrome? *(→ key)*

- High-pitched, cat-like cry in infancy.

**Concept.** Williams syndrome results from a microdeletion on chromosome **7q** that includes the

chin), an extremely friendly, overly social ("cocktail party") personality with intellectual
disability, **supravalvular aortic stenosis** (from elastin loss), and — the board trap —

- 7q microdeletion (includes the elastin gene).
- Elfin facies, extreme friendliness, intellectual disability.
- Supravalvular aortic stenosis (elastin).
- Hypercalcemia (board trap — unlike Down's normal calcium).

the supravalvular aortic stenosis.

- Williams = hypercalcemia + 7q (versus DiGeorge = hypocalcemia + 22q).
- Supravalvular (not valvular) aortic stenosis, from elastin loss.
- A microdeletion → diagnosed by FISH.

**Vignette signature.** "Elfin facies, extremely friendly child, supravalvular aortic stenosis,
hypercalcemia" → Williams.

**Mnemonic.** "Williams = 7q: **W**elcoming personality, supra**V**alvular AS, high **C**alcium."

### Prader-Willi & Angelman (15q imprinting)

```yaml
title: Prader-Willi & Angelman (15q imprinting)
category: microdeletion
system: genetics
high_yield: true
tags: [15q, imprinting, prader-willi, angelman, UBE3A, uniparental-disomy]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Both Prader-Willi and Angelman map to which chromosomal region?"
    a: "15q11-13; they differ by parent of origin (imprinting)."
  - q: "Prader-Willi results from loss of which parent's genes, and by what mechanisms?"
    a: "Loss of paternally expressed genes — paternal deletion (~70%) or maternal uniparental disomy."
  - q: "Clinical features of Prader-Willi?"
    a: "Neonatal hypotonia/poor feeding giving way to hyperphagia and obesity, short stature, hypogonadism, intellectual disability."
  - q: "Angelman results from loss of which maternally expressed gene?"
    a: "UBE3A (maternal deletion, or paternal uniparental disomy)."
  - q: "Clinical features of Angelman?"
    a: "Severe intellectual disability, an ataxic puppet-like gait, inappropriate laughter, seizures, minimal speech."
  - q: "Trap: Prader-Willi vs Angelman parent of origin?"
    a: "Prader-Willi = paternal loss; Angelman = maternal loss (same 15q region)."
references:
  - "THOMPSON §Prader-Willi and Angelman syndromes"   # primary (genetics)
```

**Concept.** These two syndromes both arise from loss of function at chromosome **15q11-13** but
differ by **parent of origin** (genomic imprinting — mechanism in Genetics — Principles &
Inheritance). **Prader-Willi** results from loss of the *paternally* expressed genes (paternal
deletion in ~70%, or maternal uniparental disomy): neonatal hypotonia and poor feeding give way
to **hyperphagia and obesity**, short stature, hypogonadism, intellectual disability, and
characteristic behaviors. **Angelman** results from loss of the *maternally* expressed **UBE3A**
(maternal deletion, or paternal uniparental disomy): severe intellectual disability, an ataxic
"puppet-like" gait, **inappropriate laughter** and a happy demeanor, seizures, and minimal speech.
Same locus, opposite parent.

**Key facts.**
- Both at 15q11-13; differ by parent of origin (imprinting).
- Prader-Willi: loss of PATERNAL genes → hyperphagia/obesity, hypotonia, hypogonadism, intellectual disability.
- Angelman: loss of MATERNAL UBE3A → ataxia, inappropriate laughter, seizures, severe intellectual disability, minimal speech.
- Mechanisms: deletion of the active-parent allele, or uniparental disomy of the silent parent.

**Clinical correlation.** This contrast is a classic board pairing; methylation testing confirms
the diagnosis and distinguishes the mechanisms.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Prader-Willi = **P**aternal loss; Angelman = m**A**ternal loss.
- Same 15q region, opposite parent of origin.

**Vignette signature.** "Obesity, hyperphagia, hypotonia, paternal 15q deletion" → Prader-Willi.
"Ataxia, inappropriate laughter, seizures, maternal 15q deletion" → Angelman.

**Mnemonic.** "**P**rader = **P**aternal; **A**ngelman = m**A**ternal."

**Check.**
1. Loss of the paternal contribution at 15q causes which syndrome? *(→ key)*
2. Loss of which maternally expressed gene underlies Angelman syndrome? *(→ key)*

**Prader-Willi vs Angelman**

| Feature | Prader-Willi | Angelman |
|---|---|---|
| Lost contribution | Paternal 15q | Maternal 15q (UBE3A) |
| Common mechanism | Paternal deletion / maternal UPD | Maternal deletion / paternal UPD |
| Hallmark | Hyperphagia, obesity, hypotonia, hypogonadism | Ataxia, inappropriate laughter, seizures |
| Speech/cognition | Intellectual disability | Severe intellectual disability, minimal speech |

**Concept.** Microdeletions are submicroscopic chromosomal losses too small to see on a standard
karyotype; they are detected by **FISH** or **chromosomal microarray**. This module consolidates
the high-yield ones into a single reference; each is detailed in its own module above.

**Key facts.**
- Detected by FISH or chromosomal microarray (not standard karyotype).
- 5p− → cri-du-chat; 7q → Williams; 15q → Prader-Willi/Angelman; 22q11.2 → DiGeorge.

**Clinical correlation.** When a syndrome is suspected clinically but the karyotype is normal, a
microdeletion is the likely explanation — order FISH/microarray.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Williams (7q) hypercalcemia vs DiGeorge (22q) hypocalcemia is the most-tested contrast.
- 15q is parent-of-origin dependent (Prader-Willi vs Angelman).

**Vignette signature.** "Clinical syndrome with a normal karyotype, confirmed by FISH" →
microdeletion.

**Check.**
1. Which laboratory technique detects microdeletions too small to see on a standard karyotype? *(→ key)*
2. A 7q microdeletion causes which syndrome? *(→ key)*

**Microdeletion summary table**

| Deletion | Syndrome | Hallmark |
|---|---|---|
| 5p− | Cri-du-chat | High-pitched cat-like cry |
| 7q | Williams | Elfin facies, supravalvular AS, hypercalcemia |
| 15q (paternal) | Prader-Willi | Hyperphagia, obesity, hypotonia |
| 15q (maternal) | Angelman | Ataxia, inappropriate laughter, seizures |
| 22q11.2 | DiGeorge | Conotruncal defect, thymic aplasia, hypocalcemia |

## Section answer key

**Down syndrome (trisomy 21)**
1. Maternal meiotic nondisjunction (risk increases with maternal age).
2. ↓AFP, ↓estriol, ↑hCG, ↑inhibin A.

**Edwards syndrome (trisomy 18)**
1. Trisomy 18 (Edwards syndrome).
2. Trisomy 18 is uniformly low (↓AFP, ↓estriol, ↓hCG, ↓/normal inhibin A), whereas trisomy 21 has
   ↑hCG and ↑inhibin A.

**Patau syndrome (trisomy 13)**
1. Trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome).
2. Usually fatal within the first year of life.

**Turner syndrome (45,X)**
1. Primary amenorrhea (the most common cause).
2. Bicuspid aortic valve and coarctation of the aorta.

**Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY)**
1. 47,XXY.
2. High (hypergonadotropic hypogonadism).

**Cri-du-chat (5p deletion)**
1. The short arm of chromosome 5 (5p−).
2. A high-pitched, cat-like cry.

**Williams syndrome (7q deletion)**
1. Chromosome 7 (7q).
2. Hypercalcemia.

**DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion)**
1. The 3rd and 4th pharyngeal pouches (forming the thymus and parathyroids).
2. Hypocalcemia.

**Prader-Willi & Angelman (15q imprinting)**
1. Prader-Willi syndrome.
2. UBE3A.

**Microdeletion summary**
1. FISH (or chromosomal microarray).
2. Williams syndrome.
