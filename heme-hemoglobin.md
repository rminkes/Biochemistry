### Heme synthesis

```yaml

category: heme metabolism
system: structural
high_yield: true

cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Rate-limiting enzyme of heme synthesis — location, substrates, and cofactor?"
    a: "ALA synthase (mitochondrial); condenses glycine and succinyl-CoA into ALA; requires vitamin B6; feedback-inhibited by heme."
  - q: "Final enzyme of heme synthesis, and what it does?"
    a: "Ferrochelatase (mitochondria) — inserts Fe²⁺ into protoporphyrin IX to make heme."
  - q: "Which two heme-synthesis enzymes does lead inhibit?"
    a: "ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase."
  - q: "What induces ALA synthase, and what suppresses it?"
    a: "Induced when heme is low (CYP-inducing drugs, fasting); suppressed by heme (and by glucose/hemin)."
  - q: "What does vitamin B6 deficiency cause via heme synthesis?"
    a: "Sideroblastic anemia (ALA synthase needs B6)."
  - q: "Trap: rate-limiting heme enzyme vs the final iron-inserting enzyme?"
    a: "ALA synthase (rate-limiting first step) vs ferrochelatase (final step)."
references:
  - "LIR §Heme synthesis"   # primary (biochemistry)
```

**Concept.** Heme is synthesized partly in mitochondria and partly in the cytosol, mainly in the
liver and erythroid precursors. The committed, rate-limiting step is **δ-aminolevulinic acid
synthase (ALA synthase)** in the mitochondrion, which condenses glycine and succinyl-CoA into
**δ-aminolevulinic acid (ALA)**; this enzyme **requires vitamin B6 (pyridoxal phosphate)** and is
feedback-inhibited by heme (and induced when heme is low — e.g., by CYP-inducing drugs). Downstream:
**ALA dehydratase** (cytosol) makes porphobilinogen, **porphobilinogen deaminase** and others build
the porphyrin ring, and the final step, **ferrochelatase** (mitochondria), inserts Fe²⁺ into
protoporphyrin IX to make heme. Two enzymes are inhibited by **lead**: ALA dehydratase and
ferrochelatase. Defects along the pathway cause the porphyrias.

**Key facts.**
- Heme synthesis spans mitochondria + cytosol; liver and erythroid precursors.
- Rate-limiting: ALA synthase (mitochondrial; glycine + succinyl-CoA → ALA; needs vitamin B6;
  heme feedback-inhibits).
- ALA dehydratase (cytosol) and ferrochelatase (mitochondria) are inhibited by lead.
- Ferrochelatase inserts Fe²⁺ into protoporphyrin IX → heme (final step).

**Clinical correlation.** ALA synthase needs vitamin B6 (B6 deficiency → sideroblastic anemia);
lead inhibits ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase; pathway defects cause the porphyrias.

**Differentiating traps.**
- ALA synthase = rate-limiting first step (mitochondria, B6); ferrochelatase = last step.
- Lead hits ALA dehydratase *and* ferrochelatase.
- Heme feedback-inhibits ALA synthase.

**Vignette signature.** "Rate-limiting enzyme of heme synthesis" → ALA synthase. "Final enzyme
inserting iron into protoporphyrin" → ferrochelatase.

**Mnemonic.** "ALA **S**ynthase **S**tarts heme; **F**errochelatase **F**inishes (adds **Fe**);
lead blocks the first dehydratase and the last (ferrochelatase)."

```yaml
    a: "Painful abdomen, Polyneuropathy, Psychological disturbances, Port-wine/dark urine, and Precipitated by drugs/fasting."
  - q: "Does acute intermittent porphyria cause photosensitivity?"
    a: "No — there is no photosensitivity (the accumulated precursors are non-cutaneous)."
  - q: "What triggers AIP attacks, and how are they treated?"
    a: "Triggers: CYP inducers, fasting, alcohol, stress (they induce ALA synthase); treat with glucose and hemin (which feedback-inhibit ALA synthase)."

    a: "Acute intermittent porphyria."
references:

```

**Concept.** Acute intermittent porphyria (AIP) is an autosomal dominant deficiency of
**porphobilinogen deaminase**, causing accumulation of the upstream precursors ALA and
porphobilinogen. Attacks are episodic and precipitated by factors that induce ALA synthase
(CYP-inducing drugs, fasting, alcohol, stress). The classic "5 P's": **P**ainful abdomen (severe,
without peritoneal signs), **P**olyneuropathy, **P**sychological disturbances, **P**ort-wine/dark
urine (darkens on standing/light), and **P**recipitated by drugs/fasting. Notably there is **no
photosensitivity** (the accumulated precursors are non-cutaneous). Treatment: remove triggers and
give **glucose and hemin**, which feedback-inhibit ALA synthase.

**Key facts.**
- AD; porphobilinogen deaminase deficiency; ↑ALA and porphobilinogen.
- Triggered by CYP inducers, fasting, alcohol, stress (which induce ALA synthase).
- 5 P's: Painful abdomen, Polyneuropathy, Psych disturbances, Port-wine urine, Precipitants.
- No photosensitivity. Treat with glucose and hemin (suppress ALA synthase).

**Clinical correlation.** Recurrent severe abdominal pain with neurologic/psychiatric symptoms and
urine that darkens, *without* skin findings; treat with glucose/hemin.

**Differentiating traps.**
- AIP = porphobilinogen deaminase; *no* skin photosensitivity (vs porphyria cutanea tarda).
- Abdominal pain + neuro/psych + darkening urine.
- Treat with glucose and hemin.

**Vignette signature.** "Recurrent abdominal pain, neuropathy, psychiatric changes, urine darkening
on standing, no skin lesions" → acute intermittent porphyria.

**Mnemonic.** "AIP = the 5 **P**'s; no photosensitivity."

```yaml
    a: "Blistering cutaneous photosensitivity on sun-exposed skin (dorsa of hands), with tea-colored urine."
```

**Concept.** Porphyria cutanea tarda (PCT) is the **most common porphyria**, caused by deficiency of
**uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase**, leading to accumulation of uroporphyrinogen, which is
photoactive. The hallmark is **blistering cutaneous photosensitivity** on sun-exposed skin
(especially the dorsa of the hands), with vesicles/bullae, fragility, and hyperpigmentation, plus
**tea-colored urine**. It is associated with hepatitis C, alcohol, estrogen, and iron overload.
There are no acute neurovisceral attacks (contrast AIP).

**Key facts.**
- Most common porphyria; uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase deficiency; ↑uroporphyrin.
- Blistering photosensitivity (sun-exposed skin, dorsa of hands); tea-colored urine.
- Associations: hepatitis C, alcohol, estrogen, iron.
- No acute neurovisceral attacks.

**Clinical correlation.** Blistering, fragile skin on the backs of the hands with sun exposure —
check for hepatitis C.

**Differentiating traps.**
- PCT = uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase; **photosensitivity/blisters** (vs AIP, no skin findings).
- Most common porphyria; hepatitis C association.

**Vignette signature.** "Blistering, fragile skin on sun-exposed hands, tea-colored urine, hepatitis
C" → porphyria cutanea tarda.

**Mnemonic.** "**PCT**: **P**hotosensitivity, **C**u**T**aneous; uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase."


**Concept.** Lead inhibits two heme-synthesis enzymes — **ALA dehydratase** and **ferrochelatase** —
producing a microcytic, hypochromic (sideroblastic-pattern) anemia with **basophilic stippling** of
red cells and elevated ALA and protoporphyrin (the ferrochelatase block raises free erythrocyte/zinc
protoporphyrin). Clinical features differ by age: **children** (lead paint, pica) —
neurodevelopmental impairment, abdominal pain, and, when severe, encephalopathy, with basophilic
stippling and possible lead lines on the gums and long-bone metaphyses; **adults** (occupational) —
peripheral neuropathy (wrist/foot drop), GI symptoms, and nephropathy. Treatment is chelation:
succimer in children; EDTA and dimercaprol for severe cases.

**Key facts.**
- Lead inhibits ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase.
- Microcytic anemia with basophilic stippling; ↑ALA, ↑protoporphyrin.
- Children: neurodevelopmental impairment/encephalopathy, abdominal pain, lead lines (gums/metaphyses).
- Adults: peripheral neuropathy (wrist/foot drop), GI symptoms, nephropathy.
- Treat: succimer (children), EDTA/dimercaprol (severe).

**Clinical correlation.** A child with developmental regression and microcytic anemia with
basophilic stippling, or a worker with wrist drop.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Lead → ALA dehydratase + ferrochelatase inhibition; basophilic stippling.
- Sideroblastic-pattern microcytic anemia (iron not low), distinct from iron-deficiency anemia.
- Succimer is the pediatric chelator.

**Vignette signature.** "Child with pica, developmental delay, microcytic anemia, and basophilic
stippling" → lead poisoning.

**Mnemonic.** "**LEAD**: **L**ead lines, **E**ncephalopathy/**E**rythrocyte stippling, **A**bdominal
pain/**A**nemia, **D**rops (wrist/foot)."

### Heme degradation & bilirubin

```yaml
title: Heme degradation & bilirubin
category: heme metabolism
system: structural
high_yield: true
tags: [bilirubin, unconjugated, conjugated, UDP-glucuronosyltransferase, kernicterus, jaundice]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Which enzyme conjugates bilirubin, and where?"
    a: "UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT), in the liver."
  - q: "Unconjugated vs conjugated bilirubin — properties?"
    a: "Unconjugated = lipophilic, albumin-bound (can cause kernicterus); conjugated = water-soluble (appears in urine)."
  - q: "Which bilirubin form causes kernicterus?"
    a: "Unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin."
  - q: "Causes of unconjugated vs conjugated hyperbilirubinemia?"
    a: "Unconjugated: hemolysis, Gilbert, Crigler-Najjar; conjugated: Dubin-Johnson, Rotor, biliary obstruction."
  - q: "What distinguishes Dubin-Johnson syndrome?"
    a: "Conjugated hyperbilirubinemia with a grossly black liver."
  - q: "Why does neonatal physiologic jaundice occur?"
    a: "Immature UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (reduced conjugation)."
references:
  - "LIR §Heme degradation"            # primary (biochemistry)
  - "ROBBINS §Jaundice and bilirubin"   # cross-reference (pathology)
```

**Concept.** Senescent red cells are broken down by splenic macrophages; heme is degraded by heme
oxygenase to biliverdin, then to **unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin**, which is lipophilic and
travels bound to albumin. In the liver, **UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT)** conjugates bilirubin
with glucuronic acid to form **conjugated (direct), water-soluble bilirubin**, excreted in bile.
Gut bacteria convert it to urobilinogen → stercobilin (brown stool), with some urobilinogen
reabsorbed and excreted in urine (urobilin, yellow). Jaundice is split by bilirubin type:
**unconjugated** hyperbilirubinemia (hemolysis, Gilbert, Crigler-Najjar — reduced conjugation) vs
**conjugated** (Dubin-Johnson, Rotor, biliary obstruction). Neonatal physiologic jaundice reflects
immature UGT; severe unconjugated bilirubin can cross the blood-brain barrier and cause kernicterus.

**Key facts.**
- RBC breakdown (splenic macrophages): heme → biliverdin → unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin
  (albumin-bound, lipophilic).
- Liver UDP-glucuronosyltransferase conjugates → conjugated (direct), water-soluble → bile.
- Gut: urobilinogen → stercobilin (stool), urobilin (urine).
- Unconjugated (hemolysis, Gilbert, Crigler-Najjar) vs conjugated (Dubin-Johnson, Rotor, obstruction).
- Neonatal jaundice (immature UGT); kernicterus from unconjugated bilirubin in the brain.

**Clinical correlation.** Distinguish unconjugated from conjugated jaundice; neonatal
jaundice/kernicterus; Gilbert syndrome (mild, provoked by fasting/stress).

**Differentiating traps.**
- Unconjugated = lipophilic, albumin-bound, can cause kernicterus; conjugated = water-soluble
  (appears in urine).
- Conjugation is by UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (deficient in Crigler-Najjar and Gilbert).
- Dubin-Johnson = conjugated, with a grossly black liver.

**Vignette signature.** "Neonate with unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia at risk of kernicterus" →
immature conjugation. "Conjugated hyperbilirubinemia with a black liver" → Dubin-Johnson.

**Mnemonic.** "**Un**conjugated = indirect, fat-soluble, brain (kernicterus); conjugated = direct,
water-soluble, urine."

**Check.**
1. Which liver enzyme conjugates bilirubin? *(→ key)*
2. Which form of bilirubin — conjugated or unconjugated — can cause kernicterus? *(→ key)*

### Hemoglobin structure & O₂ dissociation curve

```yaml
title: Hemoglobin structure & O₂ dissociation curve
category: hemoglobin
system: structural
high_yield: true
tags: [hemoglobin, cooperativity, bohr-effect, 2-3-BPG, right-shift, oxygen-dissociation-curve]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Subunit composition of adult hemoglobin (HbA), and the binding behavior?"
    a: "α₂β₂ tetramer; cooperative (sigmoidal) O₂ binding (tense ↔ relaxed states)."
  - q: "Name four factors that shift the O₂ curve right (CADET)."
    a: "↑CO₂, ↑H⁺ (low pH), ↑2,3-BPG, ↑temperature (also exercise) — the Bohr effect."
  - q: "What does a right shift mean for oxygen?"
    a: "Lower affinity → more O₂ unloading to tissues."
  - q: "What does 2,3-BPG do to hemoglobin?"
    a: "Binds and stabilizes deoxyhemoglobin (tense state), lowering affinity (right shift); it rises at altitude and in chronic hypoxia."
  - q: "Which factors shift the curve left?"
    a: "↓CO₂/↓H⁺/↓2,3-BPG/↓temperature, plus fetal Hb, CO, and methemoglobin."
  - q: "Vignette: exercise/acidosis/high CO₂ — which way does the curve shift?"
    a: "Right (more unloading)."
references:
  - "LIR §Hemoglobin"                       # primary (biochemistry)
  - "GUYTON §Oxygen transport and the dissociation curve"   # cross-reference (physiology)
```

**Concept.** Adult hemoglobin (HbA) is a tetramer of two α and two β globin chains (α₂β₂), each with
a heme binding one O₂. Oxygen binding is **cooperative** (sigmoidal curve): binding O₂ raises
affinity for subsequent O₂ (the tense → relaxed state transition). The curve **shifts right** (lower
O₂ affinity, more unloading to tissues) with increased CO₂, H⁺ (low pH), **2,3-BPG
(2,3-bisphosphoglycerate)**, and temperature — the Bohr effect — and **shifts left** (higher
affinity) with the opposite, and with fetal hemoglobin, CO, and methemoglobin. 2,3-BPG binds
deoxyhemoglobin and stabilizes the low-affinity (tense) state, promoting O₂ release; it rises at
high altitude and in chronic hypoxia.

**Key facts.**
- HbA = α₂β₂ tetramer; cooperative (sigmoidal) O₂ binding (tense ↔ relaxed states).
- Right shift (↓affinity, more unloading): ↑CO₂, ↑H⁺/↓pH, ↑2,3-BPG, ↑temperature (Bohr effect,
  exercise).
- Left shift (↑affinity): the opposite, plus fetal Hb, CO, methemoglobin.
- 2,3-BPG stabilizes the deoxy (tense) state; rises at altitude/chronic hypoxia.

**Clinical correlation.** A right shift improves tissue O₂ delivery during exercise/acidosis; 2,3-BPG
mediates altitude adaptation.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Right shift = lower affinity = more O₂ delivered to tissues.
- 2,3-BPG binds and stabilizes deoxyhemoglobin.
- Fetal Hb shifts left (next module).

**Vignette signature.** "Exercise/acidosis/high CO₂ shifting the curve" → right shift (more
unloading). "2,3-BPG effect" → right shift.

**Mnemonic.** "Right shift = **CADET** face right: **C**O₂, **A**cid, **2,3**-DPG, **E**xercise,
**T**emperature."

**Check.**
1. Name four factors that shift the oxygen dissociation curve to the right. *(→ key)*
2. What effect does 2,3-BPG have on hemoglobin's oxygen affinity? *(→ key)*

```yaml

  - q: "Why does HbF have higher O₂ affinity than HbA?"
    a: "The γ chains bind 2,3-BPG poorly, so the curve is left-shifted."
  - q: "Why do β-globin disorders present after the first few months?"
    a: "The γ→β switch (HbF → HbA) occurs over roughly the first 6 months."
  - q: "Which drug raises HbF and helps sickle cell disease?"
    a: "Hydroxyurea."
references:
  - "LIR §Hemoglobin"   # primary (biochemistry)
```

**Concept.** Fetal hemoglobin (HbF) is **α₂γ₂** (two α and two γ chains). It has **higher oxygen
affinity** than adult HbA, chiefly because the γ chains bind 2,3-BPG poorly — so the HbF curve is
left-shifted, letting the fetus extract O₂ from maternal blood across the placenta. HbF predominates
at birth and is largely replaced by HbA over the first ~6 months as γ→β switching occurs. This switch
is why β-globin disorders (sickle cell, β-thalassemia) tend to present after the first few months.
HbF — and agents that induce it, such as hydroxyurea — can ameliorate sickle cell disease.

**Key facts.**
- HbF = α₂γ₂; higher O₂ affinity (left-shifted) because γ binds 2,3-BPG poorly.
- Enables fetal O₂ extraction from maternal blood.
- γ→β switch over ~6 months → β-chain disorders manifest after infancy.
- Hydroxyurea raises HbF (helps sickle cell disease).

**Clinical correlation.** β-globin diseases are delayed until HbF declines; hydroxyurea induces HbF
in sickle cell disease.

**Differentiating traps.**
- HbF = α₂γ₂, left-shifted, low 2,3-BPG binding.
- Explains the delayed presentation of sickle cell/β-thalassemia.

**Vignette signature.** "Higher-affinity hemoglobin enabling placental O₂ transfer that declines over
the first 6 months" → fetal hemoglobin.

**Mnemonic.** "Fetal Hb = α₂γ₂; **γ** doesn't **g**rip 2,3-BPG → higher affinity (left shift)."

```yaml
  - "ROBBINS §Sickle cell disease"    # cross-reference (pathology)
```

**Concept.** Sickle cell disease is an autosomal recessive β-globin disorder from a point mutation
in which **glutamate is replaced by valine at position 6** of the β chain (Glu→Val, position 6),
producing HbS. Deoxygenated HbS polymerizes, deforming red cells into rigid sickle shapes that cause
hemolysis and vaso-occlusion. Sickling is promoted by deoxygenation, dehydration, acidosis, and
infection. Manifestations: chronic hemolytic anemia, painful vaso-occlusive crises, acute chest
syndrome, **autosplenectomy** (→ infection by encapsulated organisms such as *Streptococcus
pneumoniae*, and *Salmonella* osteomyelitis), stroke, dactylitis, and **aplastic crisis with
parvovirus B19**. Heterozygotes (sickle trait) are relatively protected against falciparum malaria.
Diagnosis is by hemoglobin electrophoresis; treatment includes hydroxyurea (which raises HbF).

**Key facts.**
- AR β-globin point mutation: Glu→Val at position 6 → HbS.
- Deoxy-HbS polymerizes → sickling, hemolysis, vaso-occlusion.
- Triggers: deoxygenation, dehydration, acidosis, infection.
- Vaso-occlusive crises, acute chest, autosplenectomy (encapsulated organisms, Salmonella
  osteomyelitis), parvovirus B19 aplastic crisis.
- Trait protects against malaria; treat with hydroxyurea (↑HbF).

**Clinical correlation.** A child with hemolytic anemia, pain crises, splenic infarction, and
infections by encapsulated organisms.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Glu→Val at position 6 (HbS) — memorize precisely.
- Autosplenectomy → encapsulated organisms; *Salmonella* osteomyelitis specifically.
- Parvovirus B19 → aplastic crisis.

**Vignette signature.** "Child with hemolytic anemia, vaso-occlusive pain crises, dactylitis, and
encapsulated-organism infections" → sickle cell disease.

**Mnemonic.** "Sickle: Glu→**Val** at position 6 (Val → sickle); autosplenectomy → encapsulated bugs."

```yaml
  - q: "How does hemoglobin C severity compare with sickle cell disease?"

```

**Concept.** Hemoglobin C disease is a β-globin variant in which **glutamate is replaced by lysine
at position 6** (Glu→Lys, position 6). HbC causes a **milder** hemolytic anemia than sickle cell;
homozygotes (HbCC) have mild chronic hemolysis with **target cells** and HbC crystals on the smear.
Compound heterozygotes with HbS (HbSC disease) have a sickling disorder that is generally milder
than HbSS but still clinically significant. The high-yield point is the position-6 contrast: **valine
→ sickle (HbS); lysine → HbC**.

**Key facts.**
- β-globin: Glu→Lys at position 6 → HbC.
- Milder hemolytic anemia; target cells and HbC crystals (homozygous HbCC).


**Concept.** Both impair oxygen delivery without necessarily lowering the dissolved PaO₂.
**Methemoglobinemia**: heme iron is oxidized from Fe²⁺ to **Fe³⁺**, which can't bind O₂ and shifts
the remaining curve left (worsening delivery); causes include oxidizing agents (dapsone, nitrites,
local anesthetics such as benzocaine). The blood is **chocolate-brown**, with cyanosis unresponsive
to oxygen; treat with **methylene blue** (and vitamin C). **CO poisoning**: carbon monoxide binds
hemoglobin with roughly 200× the affinity of O₂, forming **carboxyhemoglobin**, reducing O₂ carrying
and shifting the curve left; it presents with headache, confusion, and classically **cherry-red
skin**, often from smoke or faulty heaters, and **pulse oximetry reads falsely normal**. Treat with
100% oxygen (hyperbaric for severe cases). Both lower oxygen-carrying capacity with a normal
dissolved PaO₂.

**Key facts.**
- Methemoglobin: Fe³⁺ (oxidized) can't carry O₂; chocolate-brown blood; cyanosis unresponsive to O₂;
  from dapsone/nitrites/benzocaine. Treat: methylene blue.
- CO: binds Hb ~200× O₂ affinity → carboxyhemoglobin; cherry-red skin, headache/confusion; pulse
  oximetry falsely normal. Treat: 100% O₂ (hyperbaric if severe).
- Both: left shift, reduced O₂ delivery, normal PaO₂.

**Clinical correlation.** Methemoglobinemia after dapsone/benzocaine (brown blood, blue patient); CO
poisoning from fire/heater (cherry-red skin, falsely normal pulse oximetry).

**Differentiating traps.**
- Methemoglobin = Fe³⁺ (treat with methylene blue); CO = carboxyhemoglobin (treat with 100% O₂).
- Pulse oximetry is unreliable in both.
- Cherry-red skin (CO) vs chocolate-brown blood (methemoglobinemia).

**Vignette signature.** "Chocolate-brown blood and cyanosis unresponsive to O₂ after
benzocaine/dapsone" → methemoglobinemia. "Cherry-red skin, headache after smoke exposure, normal
pulse oximetry" → CO poisoning.

**Mnemonic.** "MetHb = Fe³⁺, chocolate blood, **M**ethylene blue; CO = **C**herry-red, 100% O₂."

## Section answer key

**Heme synthesis**
1. δ-aminolevulinic acid synthase (ALA synthase).
2. Ferrochelatase.

**Acute intermittent porphyria**
1. Porphobilinogen deaminase.
2. No — there is no photosensitivity.

**Porphyria cutanea tarda**
1. Uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase.
2. Blistering cutaneous photosensitivity on sun-exposed skin.

**Lead poisoning**
1. ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase.
2. Basophilic stippling.

**Heme degradation & bilirubin**
1. UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT).
2. Unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin.

**Hemoglobin structure & O₂ dissociation curve**
1. Any four of: increased CO₂, increased H⁺/decreased pH, increased 2,3-BPG, increased temperature
   (also exercise).
2. It decreases oxygen affinity (stabilizes the deoxy state, promoting O₂ unloading) — a right shift.

**Fetal hemoglobin**
1. α₂γ₂ (two α and two γ chains).
2. The γ chains bind 2,3-BPG poorly, so affinity is higher (a left shift).

**Sickle cell disease**
1. Glutamate → valine at position 6 of the β chain.
2. Deoxygenation (of HbS).

**Hemoglobin C**
1. Glutamate → lysine at position 6 of the β chain.
2. It is milder than sickle cell disease.

**Methemoglobinemia & CO poisoning**
1. Ferric (Fe³⁺).
2. Methylene blue.
