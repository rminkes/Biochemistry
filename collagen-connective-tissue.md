### Collagen synthesis

```yaml
title: Collagen synthesis
category: connective tissue

high_yield: true
tags: [collagen, gly-x-y, hydroxylation, vitamin-C, lysyl-oxidase, collagen-types]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "What is the repeating sequence of collagen, and why glycine every third residue?"
    a: "Gly-X-Y repeats; glycine is small enough to fit the triple-helix core."
  - q: "Which vitamin is required for hydroxylation of proline and lysine?"
    a: "Vitamin C (deficiency → scurvy)."
  - q: "Which enzyme cross-links tropocollagen extracellularly, and what cofactor does it need?"
    a: "Lysyl oxidase; it is copper-dependent."
  - q: "Intracellular vs extracellular phases of collagen synthesis?"
    a: "Intracellular: hydroxylation (vitamin C) → glycosylation → triple helix (procollagen). Extracellular: propeptide cleavage → tropocollagen → lysyl oxidase cross-linking."
  - q: "Match collagen type to location: I, II, III, IV."
    a: "I = bone/skin/tendon; II = cartilage; III = reticulin/blood vessels; IV = basement membrane."
  - q: "Why does a glycine substitution disrupt collagen?"
    a: "Glycine must occupy every third residue to fit the helix core; substituting it disrupts the helix (→ osteogenesis imperfecta)."
  - q: "Trap: vitamin C vs copper roles in collagen?"
    a: "Vitamin C for hydroxylation (intracellular); copper/lysyl oxidase for cross-linking (extracellular)."
references:
  - "LIR §Collagen synthesis"   # primary (biochemistry)
```

**Concept.** Collagen is the most abundant protein in the body, and its synthesis runs through
intracellular and extracellular phases — defects at specific steps cause distinct diseases.
**Intracellular** (fibroblast RER): synthesis of preprocollagen rich in glycine, proline, and
lysine, with **Gly-X-Y repeats** (glycine every third residue — it's small enough to fit the
triple-helix core); **hydroxylation** of proline and lysine residues (**requires vitamin C** —
deficiency causes scurvy); glycosylation; and assembly into a triple helix (procollagen).
**Extracellular**: procollagen is secreted, procollagen peptidases cleave the terminal propeptides
to form tropocollagen, and finally **lysyl oxidase** (a **copper-dependent** enzyme) cross-links
tropocollagen into mature, strong fibrils. Collagen types differ by location.

**Key facts.**
- Most abundant protein; Gly-X-Y repeats (glycine every 3rd residue fits the helix core).
- Intracellular: hydroxylation of proline/lysine (needs vitamin C) → glycosylation → triple helix
  (procollagen).
- Extracellular: propeptide cleavage (procollagen peptidase) → tropocollagen → cross-linking by
  lysyl oxidase (copper-dependent).
- Types: I (bone/skin/tendon), II (cartilage), III (reticulin/vessels), IV (basement membrane).

**Clinical correlation.** Vitamin C deficiency (scurvy) impairs hydroxylation → weak collagen
(bleeding gums, poor wound healing). Lysyl oxidase needs copper (Menkes). Specific steps/types map
to osteogenesis imperfecta, Ehlers-Danlos, and Alport (next modules).

**Differentiating traps.**
- Glycine occupies every third residue (a glycine substitution disrupts the helix → osteogenesis imperfecta).
- Vitamin C for hydroxylation (intracellular) vs copper/lysyl oxidase for cross-linking (extracellular).
- Collagen type by location (I bone, II cartilage, III vessels/reticulin, IV basement membrane).

**Vignette signature.** "Step requiring vitamin C in collagen synthesis" → hydroxylation of
proline/lysine. "Copper-dependent cross-linking enzyme" → lysyl oxidase.

**Mnemonic.** Types: "Type **I** = b**ONE** (one); Type **II** = car**TWO**lage; Type **III** =
re**THREE**culin/vessels; Type **IV** = floor (basement membrane)."

**Collagen types**

| Type | Location |
|---|---|
| I | Bone, skin, tendon (most abundant) |
| II | Cartilage |
| III | Reticulin, blood vessels, granulation tissue |
| IV | Basement membrane |

### Osteogenesis imperfecta

```yaml
title: Osteogenesis imperfecta
category: connective tissue
system: structural
inheritance: autosomal dominant (most common)

cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Which collagen type is defective in osteogenesis imperfecta, and what is the inheritance?"
    a: "Type I collagen; most commonly autosomal dominant (often a glycine substitution, dominant negative)."
  - q: "Hallmark bone finding in osteogenesis imperfecta?"
    a: "Brittle bones with multiple fractures from minimal trauma (can mimic child abuse)."
  - q: "Characteristic scleral finding in osteogenesis imperfecta?"
    a: "Blue sclerae (thin sclera revealing the underlying choroid)."
  - q: "Other features of osteogenesis imperfecta besides fractures and blue sclerae?"
    a: "Conductive hearing loss (abnormal middle-ear ossicles) and dental abnormalities."
  - q: "Vignette: child with multiple fractures, blue sclerae, and hearing loss — diagnosis?"
    a: "Osteogenesis imperfecta."
references:
  - "LIR §Collagen disorders"            # primary (biochemistry)
  - "ROBBINS §Osteogenesis imperfecta"    # cross-reference (pathology)
```

**Concept.** Osteogenesis imperfecta is most commonly an autosomal dominant defect in **type I
collagen** (COL1A1/COL1A2 — often a glycine substitution producing a dominant-negative effect).
Because type I collagen is the main collagen of bone, the hallmark is **brittle bones with multiple
fractures from minimal trauma** (which can mimic child abuse). Other features reflect type I
collagen in other tissues: **blue sclerae** (thin sclera revealing the underlying choroid),
conductive hearing loss (abnormal middle-ear ossicles), and dental abnormalities (opalescent
teeth). Severity ranges from mild to perinatally lethal.

**Key facts.**
- AD; type I collagen defect (often a glycine substitution, dominant negative).
- Brittle bones, multiple fractures with minimal trauma (mimics abuse).
- Blue sclerae, conductive hearing loss, dental abnormalities.

**Clinical correlation.** A child with recurrent fractures and blue sclerae — distinguish from
non-accidental trauma.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Type I collagen (bone); the fractures + blue sclerae + hearing loss triad.
- Easily mistaken for child abuse.

**Vignette signature.** "Child with multiple fractures, blue sclerae, and hearing loss" →
osteogenesis imperfecta.

**Mnemonic.** "OI = b**ONE** (type **I**) brittle; **B**lue sclerae."

### Ehlers-Danlos syndromes

```yaml

category: connective tissue

    a: "Hyperextensible skin, hypermobile joints (easy dislocations), and tissue fragility with easy bruising and poor wound healing."
  - q: "Which collagen type causes vascular Ehlers-Danlos, and the inheritance?"
    a: "Type III collagen (COL3A1), autosomal dominant."
  - q: "Dangerous complication of vascular Ehlers-Danlos?"
    a: "Spontaneous rupture of arteries, bowel, or the gravid uterus."
  - q: "Which collagen type do the classic/hypermobile EDS types often involve?"
    a: "Type V collagen."
  - q: "Vignette: hyperextensible skin and hypermobile joints, or arterial/organ rupture — diagnosis?"
    a: "Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (vascular type = type III collagen)."
references:
  - "LIR §Collagen disorders"        # primary (biochemistry)
  - "ROBBINS §Ehlers-Danlos syndrome" # cross-reference (pathology)
```

**Concept.** Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS) is a group of inherited collagen disorders producing
**hyperextensible skin, hypermobile joints** (easy dislocations), and tissue fragility with easy
bruising and poor wound healing. The classic and hypermobility types often involve type V collagen.
The dangerous variant is the **vascular type**, caused by a **type III collagen** defect (COL3A1,
autosomal dominant), which predisposes to spontaneous rupture of arteries, bowel, and the gravid
uterus — life-threatening. Recognizing the vascular subtype (type III collagen) is the high-yield
point.

**Key facts.**
- Heterogeneous collagen disorders: hyperextensible skin, hypermobile joints, tissue fragility/easy bruising.
- Vascular EDS = type III collagen (COL3A1) defect → arterial/organ/uterine rupture (most dangerous).
- Classic/hypermobile types often involve type V collagen.

**Clinical correlation.** A hypermobile, stretchy-skinned patient; the vascular type can present
with catastrophic vessel or organ rupture.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Vascular EDS = type III collagen (rupture risk) — the dangerous one.
- Hyperextensible skin + joint hypermobility is the general picture.

**Vignette signature.** "Hyperextensible skin and hypermobile joints; or arterial/organ rupture" →
Ehlers-Danlos (vascular type = type III collagen).

**Mnemonic.** "Vascular EDS = type **III** (**THREE** → **t**ears/rupture)."

### Alport syndrome

```yaml
title: Alport syndrome
category: connective tissue

flashcards:
  - q: "Which collagen type is defective in Alport syndrome, and the inheritance?"
    a: "Type IV collagen (basement membrane); most commonly X-linked."
  - q: "Classic triad of Alport syndrome?"
    a: "Glomerulonephritis (hematuria → renal failure), sensorineural hearing loss, and ocular abnormalities (anterior lenticonus)."
  - q: "Characteristic ocular finding in Alport syndrome?"
    a: "Anterior lenticonus (a conical lens protrusion) — NOT lens dislocation."
  - q: "GBM appearance on electron microscopy in Alport?"
    a: "Basket-weave / split lamina densa."
  - q: "Trap: Alport ocular finding vs Marfan/homocystinuria?"
    a: "Alport = anterior lenticonus (not dislocation); lens dislocation is Marfan (up) and homocystinuria (down)."
references:
  - "LIR §Collagen disorders"     # primary (biochemistry)
  - "ROBBINS §Alport syndrome"     # cross-reference (pathology)
```

**Concept.** Alport syndrome is a defect in **type IV collagen**, most commonly **X-linked**,
affecting basement membranes — especially the glomerular basement membrane (GBM), cochlea, and eye.
The classic triad: **glomerulonephritis** (hematuria progressing to renal failure), **sensorineural
hearing loss**, and **ocular abnormalities — characteristically anterior lenticonus** (a conical
protrusion of the lens) and retinal flecks. Electron microscopy of the GBM shows a
"basket-weave"/split lamina densa appearance.

**Key facts.**
- Type IV collagen defect (basement membrane); most commonly X-linked.
- Triad: glomerulonephritis (hematuria → renal failure), sensorineural hearing loss, ocular
  (anterior lenticonus).
- GBM on electron microscopy: "basket-weave"/split lamina densa.

**Clinical correlation.** Hematuria + sensorineural deafness + anterior lenticonus — "can't see,
can't hear, can't pee."

**Differentiating traps.**
- Type IV collagen = basement membrane.
- The ocular finding is anterior **lenticonus** — *not* lens dislocation (that's Marfan and
  homocystinuria).
- Most commonly X-linked.

**Vignette signature.** "Hematuria, sensorineural hearing loss, anterior lenticonus, basket-weave
GBM" → Alport.

**Mnemonic.** "Alport: can't **S**ee, **H**ear, **P**ee; type **IV** (the floor/basement membrane)."

2. What is the characteristic ocular finding in Alport syndrome? *(→ key)*

### Menkes disease

```yaml
title: Menkes disease
category: connective tissue
system: structural
enzyme: copper-transporting ATPase (ATP7A)
inheritance: X-linked recessive
high_yield: true
tags: [menkes, copper-deficiency, ATP7A, lysyl-oxidase, kinky-hair, neurodegeneration]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Menkes disease results from a defect in what, and the inheritance?"
    a: "A copper-transporting ATPase (ATP7A) → copper deficiency; X-linked recessive."
  - q: "Which cross-linking enzyme is impaired in Menkes, and why?"
    a: "Lysyl oxidase (copper-dependent) → defective collagen/elastin cross-linking."
  - q: "Characteristic features of Menkes disease?"
    a: "Kinky/brittle hair, hypotonia, neurodegeneration, fragile/tortuous vessels; low serum copper and ceruloplasmin."
  - q: "Trap: Menkes vs Wilson disease?"
    a: "Menkes = copper deficiency (kinky hair); Wilson = copper overload (liver/neuro, Kayser-Fleischer rings)."
  - q: "Vignette: infant with kinky/brittle hair, hypotonia, neurodegeneration, and low copper — diagnosis?"
    a: "Menkes disease."
references:
  - "LIR §Collagen cross-linking"   # primary (biochemistry)
```

**Concept.** Menkes disease is an X-linked recessive defect in a **copper-transporting ATPase
(ATP7A)**, causing copper deficiency. Because copper is a cofactor for **lysyl oxidase** (which
cross-links collagen and elastin) and other enzymes, the result is connective-tissue and vascular
abnormalities (tortuous, fragile arteries; aneurysms) along with the distinctive **brittle,
"kinky"/steely hair**, hypotonia, and progressive neurodegeneration. Serum copper and ceruloplasmin
are low. (Contrast Wilson disease, which is copper *overload*.)

**Key facts.**
- X-linked recessive defect in copper-transporting ATPase (ATP7A) → copper deficiency.
- Copper is needed for lysyl oxidase → defective collagen/elastin cross-linking.
- Kinky/brittle hair, hypotonia, neurodegeneration, fragile/tortuous vessels.
- Low serum copper and ceruloplasmin.

**Clinical correlation.** An infant with kinky hair, neurologic decline, and connective-tissue/
vascular fragility.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Menkes = copper **deficiency** (kinky hair); Wilson = copper **overload** (liver/neuro,
  Kayser-Fleischer rings).
- Copper deficiency impairs lysyl oxidase (cross-linking).

**Vignette signature.** "Infant with kinky/brittle hair, hypotonia, and neurodegeneration; low
copper" → Menkes.

**Mnemonic.** "**M**enkes = **M**issing copper (kinky hair); lysyl oxidase fails."

**Check.**
1. Menkes disease results from deficiency of which trace metal? *(→ key)*
2. Which collagen/elastin cross-linking enzyme is impaired as a result? *(→ key)*

### Marfan syndrome

```yaml
title: Marfan syndrome
category: connective tissue
system: structural
enzyme: fibrillin-1 (FBN1)
inheritance: autosomal dominant
high_yield: true
tags: [marfan, fibrillin-1, FBN1, lens-dislocation-up, aortic-dissection, TGF-beta]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Which gene/protein is defective in Marfan syndrome, and the inheritance?"
    a: "Fibrillin-1 (FBN1, chromosome 15); autosomal dominant."
  - q: "Is fibrillin a collagen?"
    a: "No — it's a glycoprotein of elastic microfibrils (and its loss dysregulates TGF-β signaling)."
  - q: "In which direction does the lens dislocate in Marfan?"
    a: "Upward and outward (vs downward in homocystinuria)."
  - q: "Lethal cardiovascular feature of Marfan syndrome?"
    a: "Aortic root dilation/aneurysm → aortic dissection (plus mitral valve prolapse)."
  - q: "Vignette: tall patient with arachnodactyly, upward lens dislocation, and aortic root dilation — diagnosis?"
    a: "Marfan syndrome."
references:
  - "LIR §Connective tissue disorders"   # primary (biochemistry)
  - "ROBBINS §Marfan syndrome"            # cross-reference (pathology)
```

**Concept.** Marfan syndrome is an autosomal dominant defect in **fibrillin-1 (FBN1, chromosome
15)**, a glycoprotein of elastic microfibrils (not collagen, but grouped with connective tissue).
Loss of fibrillin weakens elastic tissue and dysregulates TGF-β signaling. Features: tall stature
with long extremities, arachnodactyly, joint hypermobility, pectus deformity, and a high-arched
palate; ocular — **upward (and outward) lens dislocation** (ectopia lentis); and the lethal feature
— **aortic root dilation/aneurysm** predisposing to **aortic dissection** (plus mitral valve
prolapse). Management includes β-blockers/ARBs and serial aortic imaging.

**Key facts.**
- AD; fibrillin-1 (FBN1, chromosome 15) defect; elastic-microfibril glycoprotein; TGF-β dysregulation.
- Tall, long limbs, arachnodactyly, hypermobility, pectus, high-arched palate.
- Lens dislocation **upward/outward**; aortic root aneurysm/dissection (lethal), mitral valve prolapse.

**Clinical correlation.** A tall patient with upward lens dislocation and aortic root dilation —
dissection risk drives management.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Marfan = fibrillin-1; lens dislocates **upward** (vs **downward** in homocystinuria).
- Aortic dissection is the danger (vs thrombosis in homocystinuria).
- Fibrillin (elastic microfibrils), not collagen.

**Vignette signature.** "Tall patient with arachnodactyly, upward lens dislocation, and aortic root
dilation" → Marfan.

**Mnemonic.** "Marfan: **F**ibrillin; lens **UP**; aorta dissects."

**Check.**
1. Which gene/protein is defective in Marfan syndrome? *(→ key)*
2. In which direction does the lens dislocate in Marfan (vs homocystinuria)? *(→ key)*

### Elastin & α1-antitrypsin

```yaml
title: Elastin & α1-antitrypsin
category: connective tissue
system: structural
high_yield: true
tags: [elastin, lysyl-oxidase, alpha-1-antitrypsin, neutrophil-elastase, panacinar-emphysema, PiZZ]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "What gives elastin its stretch/recoil, and what cross-links it?"
    a: "It's rich in glycine and proline; cross-linked by lysyl oxidase (copper-dependent, shared with collagen)."
  - q: "What does α1-antitrypsin normally do?"
    a: "Inhibits neutrophil elastase, protecting elastin."
  - q: "What lung disease results from α1-antitrypsin deficiency?"
    a: "Panacinar emphysema (worsened by smoking)."
  - q: "Why does α1-antitrypsin deficiency cause liver disease?"
    a: "Misfolded protein accumulates in hepatocytes → cirrhosis (not from loss of antiprotease activity)."
  - q: "Vignette: emphysema in a young/nonsmoker with liver disease — diagnosis?"
    a: "α1-antitrypsin deficiency (classically PiZZ)."
references:
  - "LIR §Elastin"                              # primary (biochemistry)
  - "ROBBINS §α1-antitrypsin deficiency / emphysema"   # cross-reference (pathology)
```

**Concept.** Elastin gives tissues (lungs, large arteries, skin, ligaments) their stretch and
recoil; it's rich in glycine and proline and is cross-linked by **lysyl oxidase** (copper-dependent,
shared with collagen). Elastin is normally protected from breakdown by **α1-antitrypsin (A1AT)**,
which inhibits the **elastase** released by neutrophils. **α1-antitrypsin deficiency** (autosomal
codominant, classically the PiZZ genotype) leaves elastase unopposed, destroying alveolar elastin →
**panacinar emphysema** (worsened by smoking), while the misfolded protein accumulating in
hepatocytes causes **liver disease (cirrhosis)**.

**Key facts.**
- Elastin: stretch/recoil (lungs, arteries, skin); cross-linked by lysyl oxidase (copper).
- α1-antitrypsin inhibits neutrophil elastase, protecting elastin.
- α1-antitrypsin deficiency (PiZZ): panacinar emphysema (worse with smoking) + liver disease
  (misfolded protein accumulation → cirrhosis).

**Clinical correlation.** Emphysema in a young/nonsmoking patient (or accelerated with smoking)
plus liver disease → α1-antitrypsin deficiency.

**Differentiating traps.**
- α1-antitrypsin deficiency → panacinar (lower-lobe) emphysema *plus* liver disease.
- Elastin cross-linking needs lysyl oxidase/copper (shared with collagen).
- The liver injury comes from misfolded protein accumulation, not from loss of antiprotease activity.

**Vignette signature.** "Emphysema in a young/nonsmoker with liver disease" → α1-antitrypsin
deficiency.

**Mnemonic.** "α1-antitrypsin protects elastin; deficiency → emphysema **+** liver cirrhosis."

**Check.**
1. α1-antitrypsin normally inhibits which enzyme to protect lung elastin? *(→ key)*
2. Which two organ systems are affected in α1-antitrypsin deficiency? *(→ key)*
