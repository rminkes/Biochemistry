### Trinucleotide repeat disorders — overview & anticipation

```yaml
title: Trinucleotide repeat disorders — overview & anticipation
category: repeat-expansion
system: genetics
high_yield: true
tags: [trinucleotide-repeat, anticipation, CAG, CTG, CGG, GAA, polyglutamine]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "What causes trinucleotide repeat disorders?"
    a: "Expansion of an unstable trinucleotide repeat past a normal threshold."
  - q: "What is anticipation?"
    a: "Earlier onset and greater severity in successive generations as the repeats lengthen."
  - q: "Match the repeat: Huntington, myotonic dystrophy, Fragile X, Friedreich ataxia."
    a: "Huntington = CAG; myotonic dystrophy = CTG; Fragile X = CGG; Friedreich ataxia = GAA."
  - q: "How does repeat location set the mechanism (coding vs noncoding)?"
    a: "Coding/polyglutamine (Huntington) = gain of function; noncoding silencing (Fragile X, Friedreich) = loss of function."
  - q: "Parent-of-origin: which expansions tend to come through father vs mother?"
    a: "Large Huntington expansions tend to come through the father; severe congenital myotonic dystrophy and Fragile X expansions classically through the mother."
  - q: "Vignette: symptoms appear earlier and more severe each generation — phenomenon?"
    a: "Anticipation (a trinucleotide repeat disorder)."
references:
  - "THOMPSON §Repeat expansion disorders"   # primary (genetics)
```

**Concept.** A small group of disorders arises when an unstable trinucleotide repeat expands
past a normal threshold. Longer repeats are more unstable and tend to grow as they pass
through generations, producing **anticipation** — earlier onset and greater severity in
successive generations. The four classic board examples and their repeats: **Huntington
(CAG)**, **Myotonic dystrophy (CTG)**, **Fragile X (CGG)**, and **Friedreich ataxia (GAA)**.
The repeat's *location* sets the mechanism: in Huntington the CAG sits in the coding region
and encodes a toxic expanded polyglutamine tract (gain of function), whereas in Fragile X and
Friedreich the expansion is in noncoding/regulatory DNA and *silences* the gene (loss of
function). Each gets its own module below.

**Key facts.**
- Expansion of an unstable trinucleotide repeat causes disease beyond a threshold.
- Anticipation: earlier onset/greater severity each generation as repeats lengthen.
- Huntington = CAG; Myotonic dystrophy = CTG; Fragile X = CGG; Friedreich ataxia = GAA.
- Coding/polyglutamine (Huntington) = gain of function; noncoding silencing (Fragile X,
  Friedreich) = loss of function.

**Clinical correlation.** Anticipation is most striking in Huntington and myotonic dystrophy.
Parent-of-origin note: large Huntington expansions tend to come through the father; the severe
congenital myotonic dystrophy and Fragile X expansions classically come through the mother.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Match the repeat to the disease (CAG → Huntington, etc.).
- Coding polyglutamine (Huntington, gain of function) vs noncoding silencing (Fragile X,
  Friedreich, loss of function).
- Anticipation is the unifying clue across the group.

**Vignette signature.** "Symptoms appear earlier and more severe in each generation" →
trinucleotide repeat disorder (anticipation).

**Mnemonic.** Repeats by disease: **C**a**G** Huntington, **CTG** myotonic, **CGG** fragile X,
**GAA** Friedreich.

**Check.**
1. A neurologic disease shows earlier onset and worse severity in each successive generation.
   What genetic phenomenon is this? *(→ key)*
2. Which trinucleotide repeat is expanded in Huntington disease? *(→ key)*

**Trinucleotide repeat summary**

| Disease | Repeat | Gene | Location/mechanism | Inheritance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Huntington | CAG | HTT | Coding, polyglutamine (gain of function) | AD |
| Myotonic dystrophy 1 | CTG | DMPK | Noncoding, toxic RNA (spliceopathy) | AD |
| Fragile X | CGG | FMR1 | Promoter methylation silencing (loss) | X-linked |
| Friedreich ataxia | GAA | FXN | Intronic silencing of frataxin (loss) | AR |

### Fragile X syndrome

```yaml
title: Fragile X syndrome
category: repeat-expansion

cases: []

    a: "A CGG repeat expansion in FMR1 (X-linked)."
  - q: "How does the expansion cause disease in Fragile X?"
    a: "Hypermethylation silences FMR1, eliminating its protein FMRP (a loss-of-function mechanism)."
  - q: "What is Fragile X the most common cause of?"
    a: "The most common inherited cause of intellectual disability, and a leading single-gene cause of autism."
  - q: "What is the classic physical triad of Fragile X?"
    a: "Long face with a large jaw, large protruding ears, and macroorchidism."
  - q: "What is the standout physical clue in Fragile X?"
    a: "Macroorchidism (enlarged testes, especially post-pubertal)."
  - q: "What can Fragile X premutation carriers develop?"
    a: "Fragile X–associated tremor/ataxia syndrome or primary ovarian insufficiency."
  - q: "Trap: Fragile X — which repeat, and gain or loss of function?"
    a: "CGG; loss of function via methylation silencing (contrast Huntington's gain of function)."
references:
  - "THOMPSON §Fragile X syndrome"   # primary (genetics)
```

**Concept.** Fragile X is the most common *inherited* cause of intellectual disability and a
leading single-gene cause of autism, from CGG repeat expansion in the **FMR1** gene on the X
chromosome. Expansion beyond ~200 repeats hypermethylates and silences FMR1, eliminating its
protein (FMRP) needed for normal synaptic development — a loss-of-function mechanism. Being
X-linked, males are more severely affected. Classic features: a long face with a large jaw,
large protruding ears, and **macroorchidism** (enlarged testes, especially post-pubertal),
along with intellectual disability, autism-spectrum features, and mitral valve prolapse with
joint hypermobility. Premutation carriers (intermediate repeat numbers) can develop fragile
X–associated tremor/ataxia syndrome or primary ovarian insufficiency.

**Key facts.**
- CGG repeat in FMR1 (X-linked); hypermethylation silences the gene (loss of FMRP).
- Most common inherited intellectual disability; common genetic cause of autism.
- Long face, large jaw, large ears, macroorchidism; MVP, joint laxity.
- Premutation → tremor/ataxia syndrome, primary ovarian insufficiency.

**Clinical correlation.** Macroorchidism + large ears + intellectual disability is the classic
triad. Diagnosis is by repeat-length/methylation testing, not karyotype.

**Differentiating traps.**
- CGG/FMR1 (not CAG).
- Loss of function via methylation silencing (contrast Huntington's gain of function).
- Macroorchidism is the standout physical clue.

**Vignette signature.** "Boy with intellectual disability, long face, large ears, and enlarged
testes" → Fragile X.

**Mnemonic.** "Fragile X: big jaw, big ears, big testes."

### Huntington disease

```yaml
title: Huntington disease
category: repeat-expansion
system: genetics
high_yield: true
tags: [CAG, HTT, chorea, caudate, polyglutamine, autosomal-dominant]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Repeat, gene, chromosome, and inheritance of Huntington disease?"
    a: "CAG repeat in HTT on chromosome 4; autosomal dominant."
  - q: "What is the molecular mechanism in Huntington disease?"
    a: "Coding CAG → expanded polyglutamine tract → toxic gain-of-function huntingtin that kills neurons."
  - q: "Which brain structures atrophy in Huntington disease?"
    a: "The caudate and putamen (striatum); caudate atrophy enlarges the frontal horns of the lateral ventricles."
  - q: "Classic clinical triad and onset age of Huntington?"
    a: "Chorea, psychiatric disturbance, and progressive dementia; onset typically 30s–50s."
  - q: "Which neurotransmitters are lost in the Huntington striatum?"
    a: "GABA and acetylcholine."
  - q: "Trap: Huntington striatal loss involves which neurotransmitters (vs Parkinson)?"
    a: "GABA and ACh — not dopamine, which distinguishes it from Parkinson disease."
  - q: "Vignette: 40-year-old with chorea, depression, family history, caudate atrophy on MRI — diagnosis?"
    a: "Huntington disease."
references:
  - "THOMPSON §Huntington disease"   # primary (genetics)
  - "ROBBINS §Neurodegenerative diseases"   # cross-reference (pathology)
```

**Concept.** Huntington disease is an autosomal dominant neurodegeneration from CAG repeat
expansion in the **HTT** gene on chromosome 4. The CAG repeats lie in the coding region and
encode an expanded polyglutamine tract, yielding a toxic gain-of-function mutant huntingtin
that kills neurons, especially in the caudate and putamen (striatum). Onset is typically in
the 30s–50s, with **chorea** (involuntary dance-like movements), psychiatric disturbance
(depression, irritability), and progressive dementia. Imaging shows caudate atrophy with
enlarged frontal horns of the lateral ventricles, and the striatum loses GABAergic and
cholinergic neurons. Anticipation occurs, with larger (often paternally transmitted)
expansions causing earlier onset.

**Key facts.**
- CAG repeat in HTT, chromosome 4; autosomal dominant.
- Polyglutamine → toxic gain of function; caudate/putamen atrophy.
- Chorea + psychiatric changes + dementia, onset 30s–50s.
- ↓ GABA and ↓ ACh in the striatum; caudate atrophy on imaging.
- Anticipation; large expansions often paternal.

**Clinical correlation.** Mid-life chorea with personality change and a positive family history;
genetic testing quantifies the repeat. Caudate atrophy enlarges the frontal horns ("boxcar"
ventricles).

**Differentiating traps.**
- CAG (not CGG); coding-region gain of function (contrast Fragile X loss of function).
- Striatal GABA/ACh loss (not dopamine — distinguishes it from Parkinson disease).
- Autosomal dominant (appears in every generation).

**Vignette signature.** "Adult in their 30s–40s with chorea, depression, and family history;
caudate atrophy on MRI" → Huntington.

**Mnemonic.** "Huntington: **CAG** in the **C**audate, causing **C**horea."

   history. Which brain structure characteristically atrophies? *(→ key)*

### Friedreich ataxia

```yaml
title: Friedreich ataxia
category: repeat-expansion
system: genetics
high_yield: true
tags: [GAA, FXN, frataxin, ataxia, hypertrophic-cardiomyopathy, autosomal-recessive]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Repeat, gene, and inheritance of Friedreich ataxia?"
    a: "GAA repeat in FXN; autosomal recessive (the only recessive of the classic four)."
  - q: "What protein is silenced in Friedreich ataxia, and what is its role?"
    a: "Frataxin, needed for mitochondrial iron handling; deficiency causes iron accumulation and oxidative damage."
  - q: "Which tracts degenerate in Friedreich ataxia?"
    a: "Spinocerebellar tracts, dorsal columns, and corticospinal tracts."
  - q: "What is the usual cause of death in Friedreich ataxia?"
    a: "Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy."
  - q: "Neurologic exam findings in Friedreich ataxia?"
    a: "Gait/limb ataxia, loss of position and vibration sense, absent deep tendon reflexes, dysarthria — with Babinski present."
  - q: "Vignette: teenager with progressive ataxia, absent reflexes, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy — diagnosis?"
    a: "Friedreich ataxia."
references:
  - "THOMPSON §Friedreich ataxia"   # primary (genetics)
```

**Concept.** Friedreich ataxia is the exception among the classic repeats — **autosomal
recessive**, from a GAA expansion in the **FXN** gene that silences **frataxin**, a protein
needed for mitochondrial iron handling. Frataxin deficiency causes mitochondrial iron
accumulation and oxidative damage, degenerating the spinocerebellar tracts, dorsal columns,
and corticospinal tracts. Children and adolescents present with progressive gait and limb
ataxia, loss of position and vibration sense with absent deep tendon reflexes, and dysarthria
— and, high yield, **hypertrophic cardiomyopathy** (the usual cause of death) plus a high rate
of diabetes mellitus and kyphoscoliosis, often with pes cavus.

**Key facts.**
- GAA repeat in FXN; autosomal recessive; silences frataxin (mitochondrial).
- Spinocerebellar + dorsal column + corticospinal tract degeneration.
- Ataxia, loss of vibration/position sense, areflexia, dysarthria; Babinski present.
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (cause of death), diabetes, kyphoscoliosis, pes cavus.

**Clinical correlation.** A child/teen with ataxia, absent reflexes, and cardiomyopathy. The
cardiomyopathy — not the neurologic disease — is usually fatal.

**Differentiating traps.**
- GAA, autosomal recessive — the only recessive of the classic four.
- Frataxin → mitochondrial iron; hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is the lethal feature.
- Areflexia *with* an upgoing toe (Babinski) — combined tract involvement.

**Vignette signature.** "Teenager with progressive ataxia, absent reflexes, and hypertrophic
cardiomyopathy" → Friedreich ataxia.

**Mnemonic.** "**Fr**iedreich = **Fr**ataxin — staggers, with a big (hypertrophic) heart."

**Check.**
1. A teenager has progressive ataxia, loss of vibration sense, absent reflexes, and
   hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Diagnosis? *(→ key)*
2. Friedreich ataxia is the only one of the classic four repeat disorders with which
   inheritance pattern? *(→ key)*

### Myotonic dystrophy

```yaml
title: Myotonic dystrophy type 1
category: repeat-expansion
system: genetics
high_yield: true
tags: [CTG, DMPK, myotonia, cataracts, frontal-balding, spliceopathy, autosomal-dominant]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "Repeat, gene, and inheritance of myotonic dystrophy type 1?"
    a: "CTG repeat in DMPK; autosomal dominant."
  - q: "What is the molecular mechanism of myotonic dystrophy type 1?"
    a: "The toxic CUG-containing RNA sequesters the splicing regulator MBNL, mis-splicing many transcripts (a spliceopathy)."
  - q: "What is myotonia?"
    a: "Delayed muscle relaxation — e.g., being unable to release a handshake."
  - q: "Is the muscle weakness in myotonic dystrophy proximal or distal early on?"
    a: "Distal."
  - q: "Multisystem features of myotonic dystrophy type 1?"
    a: "Early cataracts, frontal balding, gonadal atrophy, cardiac conduction defects, and insulin resistance."
  - q: "Vignette: adult who can't release a grip after a handshake, with early cataracts and frontal balding — diagnosis?"
    a: "Myotonic dystrophy (type 1)."
references:
  - "THOMPSON §Myotonic dystrophy"   # primary (genetics)
```

**Concept.** Myotonic dystrophy type 1 is autosomal dominant, from a CTG repeat expansion in
the **DMPK** gene. The expanded CUG-containing RNA is itself toxic — it sequesters the splicing
regulator MBNL, mis-splicing many transcripts (a spliceopathy; see Molecular Biology). The
hallmark is **myotonia** — delayed muscle relaxation, such as being unable to release a
handshake — with progressive *distal* muscle weakness and wasting. The multisystem features
are classic board material: early cataracts, frontal balding, gonadal atrophy/testicular
failure, cardiac conduction defects (arrhythmias), and insulin resistance. Anticipation is
marked, with the severe congenital form usually transmitted maternally.

**Key facts.**
- CTG repeat in DMPK; autosomal dominant; toxic RNA sequesters MBNL (spliceopathy).
- Myotonia (delayed relaxation) + distal weakness and wasting.
- Cataracts, frontal balding, gonadal atrophy, cardiac conduction defects, insulin resistance.
- Strong anticipation; severe congenital form often maternally transmitted.

**Clinical correlation.** A patient who can't release a grip after a handshake, with cataracts
and arrhythmias. Cardiac conduction disease warrants monitoring.

**Differentiating traps.**
- CTG (not CAG); RNA-mediated spliceopathy mechanism.
- Myotonia = delayed relaxation (not spasticity).
- *Distal* (not proximal) weakness early.

**Vignette signature.** "Adult who can't let go after a handshake, with early cataracts and
frontal balding" → myotonic dystrophy.

**Mnemonic.** "Myotonic dystrophy: can't let **go** (myotonia); **CTG**."

**Check.**
1. An adult cannot relax their grip after a handshake and has early cataracts and frontal
   balding. Diagnosis? *(→ key)*
2. Which trinucleotide repeat is expanded in myotonic dystrophy type 1? *(→ key)*

### Oncogenes

```yaml
title: Oncogenes
category: cancer genetics
system: genetics
high_yield: true
tags: [oncogene, gain-of-function, RAS, MYC, HER2, BCR-ABL, BCL-2, translocation]
cases: []
flashcards:
  - q: "How many alleles must be activated for an oncogene, and what kind of change?"
    a: "One — a gain of function, dominant at the cell level."
  - q: "By what mechanisms are oncogenes activated?"
    a: "Point mutation, gene amplification, translocation, or overexpression."
  - q: "RAS — what kind of protein and what alteration?"
    a: "A GTPase locked in the active (on) state by point mutation → many carcinomas."
  - q: "MYC — translocation and associated cancer?"
    a: "t(8;14) → Burkitt lymphoma."
  - q: "HER2/neu — alteration, cancer, and targeted therapy?"
    a: "Amplification → aggressive breast cancer; targeted by trastuzumab."
  - q: "BCR-ABL — translocation, cancer, and drug?"
    a: "t(9;22) Philadelphia chromosome → CML; a constitutive tyrosine kinase targeted by imatinib."
  - q: "Match translocations: t(9;22), t(8;14), t(14;18), t(15;17)."
    a: "t(9;22)→CML, t(8;14)→Burkitt, t(14;18)→follicular lymphoma, t(15;17)→acute promyelocytic leukemia."
references:
  - "ROBBINS §Neoplasia"                  # primary (pathology)
  - "KATZUNG §Targeted anticancer agents" # cross-reference (pharmacology)
```

**Concept.** Oncogenes are mutated or overexpressed versions of normal proto-oncogenes (genes
that promote growth). Unlike tumor suppressors, only **one** allele needs activating — a gain
of function that is dominant at the cell level. Activation occurs by point mutation (RAS), gene
amplification (HER2/neu, N-MYC), translocation (BCR-ABL, MYC), or overexpression. High-yield
examples: **RAS** (a GTPase locked "on" → many carcinomas), **MYC** (t(8;14) → Burkitt
lymphoma), **HER2/neu** (amplified → aggressive breast cancer, targeted by trastuzumab),
**BCR-ABL** (t(9;22) Philadelphia chromosome → CML, a constitutive tyrosine kinase targeted by
imatinib), and **BCL-2** (t(14;18) → follicular lymphoma, anti-apoptotic). Many are therapeutic
targets — the "so what" layer.

**Key facts.**
- Gain of function; ONE activated allele suffices (dominant at the cell level).
- Activation by point mutation, amplification, translocation, or overexpression.
- RAS → carcinomas; MYC t(8;14) → Burkitt; HER2/neu → breast (trastuzumab).
- BCR-ABL t(9;22) → CML (imatinib); BCL-2 t(14;18) → follicular lymphoma (anti-apoptotic).

**Clinical correlation.** Targeted therapies exploit specific oncogenes — trastuzumab (HER2),
imatinib (BCR-ABL), and others.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Oncogene = ONE hit, gain of function (vs tumor suppressor = two hits, loss).
- Match translocation to cancer: t(9;22)→CML, t(8;14)→Burkitt, t(14;18)→follicular,
  t(15;17)→acute promyelocytic leukemia.
- BCL-2 works by blocking apoptosis, not by driving proliferation directly.

**Vignette signature.** "t(9;22) Philadelphia chromosome, treated with imatinib" → BCR-ABL/CML.
"HER2-amplified breast cancer, trastuzumab" → HER2/neu. "t(8;14), 'starry-sky' lymphoma" →
MYC/Burkitt.

**Check.**
1. A leukemia carries the t(9;22) translocation, creating a constitutively active tyrosine
   kinase. Name the oncogene fusion. *(→ key)*
2. How many alleles must be altered to activate an oncogene? *(→ key)*

**Oncogene summary**

| Oncogene | Alteration | Associated tumor | Targeted therapy |
|---|---|---|---|
| RAS | Point mutation (GTPase stuck on) | Many carcinomas | (various, indirect) |
| MYC | t(8;14) translocation | Burkitt lymphoma | — |
| HER2/neu | Amplification | Breast (aggressive) | Trastuzumab |
| BCR-ABL | t(9;22), Philadelphia | CML | Imatinib |
| BCL-2 | t(14;18), anti-apoptotic | Follicular lymphoma | — |

### Tumor suppressor genes

```yaml

    a: "Both (two-hit, loss of function, recessive at the cell level)."
  - q: "TP53 — chromosome, nickname, and significance?"
    a: "Chromosome 17; the guardian of the genome; the most commonly mutated gene in human cancer; germline loss = Li-Fraumeni."
  - q: "RB1 — chromosome, function, and tumors?"
    a: "Chromosome 13; brakes the G1→S transition; retinoblastoma and osteosarcoma."
  - q: "APC — what syndrome and consequence?"
    a: "Familial adenomatous polyposis → hundreds of colon polyps and near-certain colon cancer."
  - q: "What is the normal function of BRCA1/2?"
    a: "Homologous recombination DNA repair (hereditary breast/ovarian cancer)."
  - q: "What is NF1's gene product and its action?"
    a: "Neurofibromin, a RAS-GAP that turns RAS off; losing it leaves RAS unchecked."
  - q: "Vignette: family with sarcomas, breast and brain tumors, and adrenocortical carcinoma — syndrome/gene?"
    a: "Li-Fraumeni syndrome (germline TP53)."
references:
  - "ROBBINS §Neoplasia"        # primary (pathology)
  - "THOMPSON §Cancer genetics"  # cross-reference (genetics)
```

**Concept.** Tumor suppressors normally brake cell division or trigger apoptosis; cancer arises
when **both** copies are lost (two-hit, loss of function, recessive at the cell level — see Loss
of Heterozygosity). Key examples: **TP53** (chromosome 17; the "guardian of the genome," it
arrests the cycle and triggers apoptosis after DNA damage; the most commonly mutated gene in
human cancer; germline loss = Li-Fraumeni syndrome). **RB1** (chromosome 13; restrains the
G1→S transition; retinoblastoma and osteosarcoma). **APC** (chromosome 5; familial adenomatous
polyposis → hundreds of colon polyps and near-certain colon cancer). **BRCA1/2** (homologous
recombination DNA repair; hereditary breast/ovarian cancer). **NF1** (neurofibromin, a RAS-GAP;
neurofibromatosis type 1). **VHL** (renal cell carcinoma, hemangioblastomas). **WT1** (Wilms
tumor).

**Key facts.**
- Loss of function; BOTH alleles must be inactivated (two-hit).
- TP53 (17): guardian of the genome; most commonly mutated; Li-Fraumeni.
- RB1 (13): G1→S brake; retinoblastoma, osteosarcoma.
- APC (5): FAP → colon cancer.
- BRCA1/2: homologous recombination repair; breast/ovarian.
- NF1: neurofibromin (RAS-GAP); VHL: RCC/hemangioblastoma; WT1: Wilms.

**Clinical correlation.** Li-Fraumeni (germline TP53) → early sarcomas, breast, brain, and
adrenocortical tumors. FAP → prophylactic colectomy. BRCA → PARP inhibitors and risk-reducing
surgery.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Tumor suppressor = two hits, loss (vs oncogene = one hit, gain).
- TP53 = most commonly mutated; RB1 = the original two-hit gene.
- NF1's product is a RAS-GAP (turns RAS off); losing it leaves RAS unchecked.

**Vignette signature.** "Family with sarcomas, breast and brain tumors, and adrenocortical
carcinoma" → Li-Fraumeni/TP53. "Hundreds of colon polyps in youth" → APC/FAP.

**Check.**
1. Which tumor suppressor, the "guardian of the genome," is the most commonly mutated gene in
   human cancer? *(→ key)*
2. Germline mutation of which gene causes familial adenomatous polyposis with hundreds of
   colon polyps? *(→ key)*

**Tumor suppressor summary**

| Gene | Chromosome | Normal function | Syndrome / tumor |
|---|---|---|---|
| TP53 | 17 | Cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis | Li-Fraumeni; most cancers |
| RB1 | 13 | G1→S brake | Retinoblastoma, osteosarcoma |
| APC | 5 | Restrains WNT/proliferation | FAP, colon cancer |
| BRCA1/2 | 17/13 | Homologous recombination repair | Breast/ovarian cancer |
| NF1 | 17 | Neurofibromin (RAS-GAP) | Neurofibromatosis type 1 |
| VHL | 3 | Degrades HIF | RCC, hemangioblastoma |
| WT1 | 11 | Kidney/gonad development | Wilms tumor |

### Pharmacogenetics

```yaml
title: Pharmacogenetics
category: pharmacogenetics

high_yield: true
tags: [G6PD, pseudocholinesterase, acetylator, TPMT, CYP, malignant-hyperthermia, HLA]

flashcards:
  - q: "G6PD deficiency — inheritance and consequence?"

    a: "Myelosuppression from thiopurines (azathioprine, 6-mercaptopurine)."
  - q: "RYR1 mutations cause what, and what is the treatment?"
    a: "Malignant hyperthermia (with inhaled anesthetics or succinylcholine); treat with dantrolene."
  - q: "Trap: prolonged paralysis after succinylcholine vs rigidity + hyperthermia + rising CO₂ under anesthesia?"
    a: "Prolonged paralysis = pseudocholinesterase deficiency; rigidity/hyperthermia/rising CO₂ = malignant hyperthermia (RYR1)."
  - q: "Which HLA alleles pair with abacavir and carbamazepine reactions?"
    a: "HLA-B*57:01 (abacavir hypersensitivity); HLA-B*15:02 (carbamazepine Stevens-Johnson syndrome)."
  - q: "Vignette: hemolysis after an antimalarial in a Mediterranean or African male — deficiency?"
    a: "G6PD deficiency."
references:
  - "KATZUNG §Pharmacogenomics"   # primary (pharmacology)
  - "THOMPSON §Pharmacogenetics"   # cross-reference (genetics)
```

**Concept.** Pharmacogenetics studies inherited variation in drug response — high yield because
a "normal" dose can harm a genetically distinct patient. Classic associations: **G6PD
deficiency** (X-linked) → hemolysis with oxidant drugs (primaquine, dapsone, sulfonamides,
nitrofurantoin), fava beans, or infection. **Atypical pseudocholinesterase (butyrylcholinesterase)
deficiency** → prolonged paralysis after succinylcholine. **Slow acetylators (NAT2)** accumulate
drugs like isoniazid, hydralazine, and procainamide, predisposing to drug-induced lupus and
isoniazid neuropathy. **TPMT deficiency** → myelosuppression from thiopurines (azathioprine,
6-mercaptopurine). **CYP2D6/CYP2C19** variants alter activation/clearance of many drugs (codeine,
clopidogrel). **RYR1** mutations → malignant hyperthermia (massive Ca²⁺ release with inhaled
anesthetics or succinylcholine; treat with dantrolene). **HLA** associations: HLA-B*57:01
(abacavir hypersensitivity) and HLA-B*15:02 (carbamazepine Stevens-Johnson syndrome in certain
populations).

**Key facts.**
- G6PD deficiency (X-linked) → oxidant-drug hemolysis (primaquine, dapsone, sulfa).
- Pseudocholinesterase deficiency → prolonged succinylcholine paralysis.
- Slow acetylators (NAT2) → isoniazid/hydralazine/procainamide lupus & INH neuropathy.
- TPMT deficiency → thiopurine (azathioprine/6-MP) myelosuppression.
- CYP2D6/2C19 → codeine and clopidogrel activation variability.
- RYR1 → malignant hyperthermia (treat with dantrolene).
- HLA-B*57:01 abacavir; HLA-B*15:02 carbamazepine SJS.

**Clinical correlation.** Pre-treatment testing prevents serious reactions: HLA-B*57:01 before
abacavir, TPMT before thiopurines, G6PD before primaquine or rasburicase.

**Differentiating traps.**
- Prolonged paralysis after succinylcholine = pseudocholinesterase deficiency; rigidity +
  hyperthermia + rising CO₂ under anesthesia = malignant hyperthermia (RYR1).
- Slow acetylators → drug-induced lupus (hydralazine, procainamide, isoniazid).
- G6PD is X-linked; hemolysis is triggered by oxidant stress.

**Vignette signature.** "Prolonged apnea after succinylcholine" → pseudocholinesterase
deficiency. "Fever, rigidity, rising end-tidal CO₂ under anesthesia" → malignant hyperthermia
(dantrolene). "Hemolysis after an antimalarial in a Mediterranean or African male" → G6PD.

2. Under general anesthesia a patient develops muscle rigidity, hyperthermia, and rising
